The Strangest Instruments in Canadian Music

Canadian musicians have never been precious about what makes a sound — only about whether it works. Across the country's discography you'll find medieval machines, electronic ghosts, and orchestral plumbing pressed into service on pop records. The oddest instruments in the Canadian canon, and how they got there:

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY

The strange-instrument canon

  1. The hurdy-gurdy (Arcade Fire) — A medieval wheel-and-string contraption that sounds like a cross between a bagpipe and an accordion, played by cranking a wheel while pressing keys. Arcade Fire dragged one onto Funeral-era arrangements? — keep it clean: Arcade Fire wove the hurdy-gurdy into their orchestral-indie arsenal, where its drone became part of the band's signature enormity. Nothing says "orchestral reset" like a 500-year-old folk machine.
  2. The theremin (various) — The hands-in-the-air electronic instrument whose eerie wail sci-fi fans know from every 50s spaceship — adopted by Canadian experimenters from the electronic underground to art-pop auteurs. It looks like magic; it plays like a nightmare; on the right record, it's perfect.
  3. Pipe organs (church-rock recordings) — When Arcade Fire bought a deconsecrated church for Neon Bible, the building's pipe organ became the album's gravitational center — literally turning sacred plumbing into a rock instrument (see our Neon Bible-era coverage). The tradition runs deep: churches across the country have doubled as recording studios precisely because of what those pipes do to a tape.
  4. The waterphone (film-score circles) — The spiky stainless-steel bowl that produces whale-like, horror-movie wails when bowed — a staple of Canadian composers' film and game work, where its impossible tones score the country's many cinematic wildernesses.
  5. Prepared piano (the jazz and art worlds) — Objects inserted between piano strings — the Cage tradition — turning the nation's most common instrument into a percussion forest. Canadian jazz and new-music players have long treated the piano as raw material, not a finished object.
  6. The Chapman Stick and tapping instruments — The neck-tapped guitar-bass hybrid that lets one player cover an entire rhythm section — adopted by Canadian prog and fusion players as a way to be the whole band at once.
  7. Bagpipes outside the parade — From Celtic-rock fusions to punk-adjacent noise experiments, the pipes keep escaping the ceremonies and invading records they weren't invited to — which is, of course, their ideal use.
  8. Folk machines: autoharp, dulcimer, musical saw — The kitchen-party instrument shelf — autoharps, hammered dulcimers, and the singing musical saw — surfaces across Canada's folk and roots records, where "strange" is just "grandmother's."

Why strange instruments work here

Canada's musical culture is craft-first, preciousness-never: the question is never "is this instrument proper?" but "does this sound belong?" A band that will record in a barn, a church, or a cabin (see our studio features) will not hesitate to hand a medieval crank instrument to its multi-instrumentalist. The strangeness is the sound of a scene that treats all sound as available.

Frequently asked questions

What unusual instruments has Arcade Fire used?

Most famously the hurdy-gurdy (a medieval droning string instrument) and the pipe organ of their deconsecrated-church studio on Neon Bible.

What is a waterphone?

A spiked stainless-steel resonator that produces haunting, whale-like tones when bowed or struck — a favorite of film composers.

Why do Canadian musicians experiment with instruments?

A craft-first, DIY-rooted culture treats all sound as available material — if it makes the right noise, it belongs on the record, whatever it is.
