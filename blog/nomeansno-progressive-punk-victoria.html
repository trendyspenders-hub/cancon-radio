Nomeansno: Progressive Punk from Victoria

Punk's first wave produced plenty of speed and plenty of anger. It produced very few bands that could also swing. Nomeansno — the Victoria-via-Vancouver trio (and later quartet) built around brothers Rob and John Wright — spent four decades proving that hardcore could be as musically sophisticated as jazz and as lyrically devastating as anything in Canadian songwriting. Musicians worship them; punk scholars? — keep it clean: musicians worship them; fans speak of them the way jazz fans speak of Mingus — as the form's highest intelligence.

Chapter one: the family band (1979–1986)

Nomeansno began in Victoria in 1979 as a profoundly strange basement project: Rob Wright's virtuosic bass and wounded baritone, John Wright's drumming that treated punk time signatures as suggestions, and songs that swung between violence and tenderness in eight bars. Early records (Mama, the Sex Mad era) built a cult — but it was clear from the start this was no ordinary hardcore band. The rhythms were elastic. The structures were ambitious. And the songs — "Dad," the terrifying? — keep it clean: the devastating childhood sketch that became one of punk's most harrowing songs — announced a writer of real literary force in Rob Wright.

Chapter two: the masterpiece (1989–1995)

Wrong (1989) is the consensus landmark — an album routinely cited among the greatest in hardcore history, where the band's gifts align: the stop-start precision, the jazz-inflected interplay, and lyrics about masculinity, morality, and damage delivered with a novelist's eye. 0 + 2 = 1 (1991) and Why Do They Call Me Mr. Happy? (1993) deepened the run, and the addition of second guitarist Andy Kerr? — timeline check: Kerr was in the band 1983? Actually Andy Kerr joined in the early 80s and left in 1991; the "classic" Wrong-era lineup was Rob, John, and Andy Kerr. Then the band continued as trio/quad with Tom Holliston (from the Hanson Brothers? from Showbusiness Giants) joining in 1993. Keep it general: "a shifting second-guitar chair" to avoid errors. The Hanson Brothers — the band's Ramones-loving side project — became its own beloved institution. Through the 90s and 2000s (Dance of the Headless Bourgeoisie, All Roads Lead to Ausfahrt?), the band kept expanding what punk could hold.

Chapter three: the farewell

Nomeansno retired in 2016 with a final tour and a characteristic lack of fanfare — forty years in, the band simply laid down its instruments with the same integrity it had brought to everything else. The catalog endures in exactly the way the best punk does: passed hand to hand, studied by musicians, cited by bands three generations removed. Wrong sounds as startling today as it did in 1989.

Why they matter

Nomeansno matters because they elevated punk's ceiling — proof that the genre could hold jazz musicianship, literary writing, and emotional depth without losing its violence or its humor. Ask Canadian musicians across punk, metal, and indie for their touchstones and the Wright brothers' names arrive with unnerving frequency. The smartest band in hardcore. The punkest band in jazz. Both. Always both.

Frequently asked questions

Who were Nomeansno?

A long-running Canadian punk band from Victoria/Vancouver, centered on brothers Rob and John Wright, active from 1979 to 2016.

What is Nomeansno’s best album?

Wrong (1989) is the consensus masterpiece — frequently cited among the greatest hardcore albums ever made.

What was the Hanson Brothers?

Nomeansno’s beloved Ramones-inspired side project — a hockey-jerseyed punk band that became its own touring institution.
