The Irish Rovers: "The Unicorn" and the Long Tail

In 1968, a group of Irish immigrants playing Toronto's folk clubs recorded a Shel Silverstein novelty song about the end of the unicorns, and accidentally built a sixty-year institution. The Irish Rovers — Toronto-founded by Irish newcomers — became the house band of North America's Irish identity: their "The Unicorn" a generational singalong, their St. Patrick's Day television specials a holiday tradition, their six-decade career a masterclass in the long tail — the career that doesn't peak so much as endure.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the Toronto formation (1963)

The Rovers formed in Toronto in 1963 — Irish musicians newly arrived in the folk-boom city (see our 60s feature) — and did what immigrant musicians do: carried the homeland's repertoire into the new land's venues. The folk-club circuit embraced them; the recordings followed; and 1968's The Unicorn delivered the phenomenon: Silverstein's whimsical end-times ballad ("green alligators and long-necked geese, some humpty-backed camels and some chimpanzees") became a massive international hit — a children's anthem, a pub singalong, a campfire standard, all at once. The Rovers had their immortality in the bank, sixty years early.

Chapter two: the institution years

The decades that followed made the case that "The Unicorn" was no novelty fluke but the foundation of a tradition: the annual St. Patrick's Day television specials (a Canadian holiday institution for years), the albums mining the Irish songbook with genuine musicianship and show-business polish, the touring that never stopped — the Rovers built the Irish-North-American entertainment franchise: part band, part heritage ceremony, part beloved uncle? — keep it light: part band, part heritage ceremony, entirely reliable. The lineup evolved (the band's multi-generation structure becoming a family enterprise), the audience renewed itself (the children's-choir chorus of "The Unicorn" training each new generation of singers), and the catalog kept compounding: drinking songs, rebel songs, comic songs, lullabies — the full Irish-emigrant repertoire, professionally maintained.

Chapter three: the long tail, measured

By any metric the industry respects — chart peaks, critical canon, hip cachet — the Rovers are a footnote. By the metric that actually matters — how long a song stays sung — they're giants: "The Unicorn" has never left circulation, the specials live in the culture's holiday memory, and the band's six-decade run outlasted nearly every institution formed the same year. The long tail isn't a consolation prize. It's the whole game, and the Rovers play it better than almost anyone in Canadian music. Long-necked geese, forever.

Why they matter

They matter because the heritage-band tradition — music as community memory, performed professionally and loved generationally — is one of the country's quietest success stories, and the Rovers are its emblem: the Toronto immigrants who made themselves a national holiday. The unicorns missed the ark. The Rovers made the boat. Sixty years and counting.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the Irish Rovers?

A Toronto-founded band of Irish musicians (formed 1963) — the house band of North American Irish identity, famous for “The Unicorn.”

What is “The Unicorn”?

The Rovers’ 1968 signature hit — Shel Silverstein’s whimsical Noah’s Ark ballad, a perennial children’s and pub singalong.

Are the Irish Rovers still together?

Yes — the band continues to perform and record after six decades, a family enterprise and St. Patrick’s Day institution.
