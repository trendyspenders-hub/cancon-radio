Newfoundland's Folk Revival: The Kitchen Party Conquers the Country

Newfoundland's traditional music never needed saving — it needed a road out. For generations the island's songs lived exactly where they were born: in kitchens, at kitchen parties, in the pubs of St. John's where a Friday night singalong could include a fisherman, a student, and a future superstar. What changed in the 1990s was scale. The Newfoundland folk revival took the kitchen party's spirit — communal, rowdy, unselfconscious — and plugged it into amplifiers, and the result, Great Big Sea, became one of the biggest bands in Canadian history. This is how the island taught the mainland to sing.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the tradition

Newfoundland's songbook is older than the country — sea shanties, Irish and West Country ballads, Gaelic airs, and original compositions, all crossbred on the Rock into something distinct. The tradition survived because it was functional: songs for work, for wake nights, for the long winter. The kitchen party — the island's legendary house-party institution, where the furniture gets pushed back and everyone sings — kept the repertoire alive and communal. By the 1970s, the tradition had its modern champions (the island's folk institutions and festival culture were already strong), and the stage was set for a generation that would treat the old songs as raw material rather than relic.

Chapter two: the revival juggernaut (1993–2000)

Great Big Sea — Alan Doyle, Séan McCann, Bob Hallett, and Darrell Power — formed in St. John's in 1993 out of the city's pub scene, where they learned the revival's core skill: making three hundred strangers feel like one kitchen party. The formula was fearless: traditional Newfoundland songs and tunes given electric-rock muscle, original songs in the tradition's voice, and live shows that were less concerts than participatory events. The albums — the self-titled debut (1993), Up (1995) with "Rant and Roar," Turn (1999) with "Ordinary Day" and the double-time "Mari-Mac" — stacked up platinum certifications and Juno wins. Great Big Sea became the rarest of things: a folk band that filled arenas, and a Newfoundland band that belonged to the whole country.

Chapter three: the legacy and the diaspora

The band's second act was as generational custodians: Séan McCann's departure (2013) and the group's subsequent wind-down closed one era, but the revival's infrastructure only grew. Alan Doyle became a solo star and multimedia fixture, the tradition's most charming ambassador; McCann continued as a solo artist and an open advocate for healing through music; and the island's scene — the pubs of George Street, the festivals, the trad collectives — kept exporting the kitchen party worldwide. Younger Newfoundland acts now inherit a playing field their predecessors built: the world already knows the island sings, and expects to be invited.

Why it matters

The Newfoundland revival proved that Canada's oldest regional music wasn't heritage — it was ammunition. Great Big Sea took songs meant for a kitchen and made them arena anthems without sanding off a single rough edge, and in doing so gave the country one of its great collective experiences: hundreds of thousands of mainlanders, many with no Newfoundland connection at all, bellowing trad lyrics like they were born on the Bonavista Peninsula. That's not revival. That's conquest — the friendliest in Canadian music history.

Frequently asked questions

Who are Great Big Sea?

A Newfoundland trad-rock band (Alan Doyle, Séan McCann, Bob Hallett, Darrell Power) formed in St. John’s in 1993 — multi-platinum, Juno-winning, and one of the biggest live acts in Canadian history.

What is the Newfoundland folk revival?

The 1990s wave that brought the island’s traditional songs and kitchen-party spirit to mainstream stages, led by Great Big Sea and sustained by the St. John’s pub scene and festival culture.

Is Great Big Sea still together?

The band wound down after Séan McCann’s 2013 departure; the members continue in solo careers, and the revival’s tradition continues through Newfoundland’s vibrant scene.
