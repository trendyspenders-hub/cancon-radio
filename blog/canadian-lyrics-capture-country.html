Ten Canadian Lyrics That Perfectly Capture the Country

Some lines don't just describe Canada — they are Canada, compressed. Ten of them (the choice is the argument), ranked by how much country they hold:

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY

The ten

  1. "You could drink a case of you and still be on my feet." — Joni Mitchell, "A Case of You" (1971). The devotion rendered in winter imagery (the skating, the escape, the northern metaphor): love as something you'd brave a frozen river for. The specific made universal (see our features).
  1. "I hate Winnipeg." — The Weakerthans, "One Great City!" (2003). The civic love-song disguised as an insult (see our Weakerthans feature): every detail of the song argues with the refrain until the affection wins. The most honest civic lyric ever sung (see our Winnipeg feature).
  1. "The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald / Does anyone know where the love of God goes / When the waves turn the minutes to hours?" — Gordon Lightfoot (1976). Twenty-nine men, a lake, and a question that is the Canadian relationship with the water (see our Lightfoot feature): reverence, labor, and the vast indifferent north.
  1. "Mon pays, ce n'est pas un pays, c'est l'hiver." — Gilles Vigneault, "Mon pays" (1964). The country defined by its season (see our Vigneault feature): not a flag, not a border — winter. The most precise national sentence ever sung (see our name-dropping feature).
  1. "We're not the ones you read about in history books." — The Tragically Hip, "Courage" (1992). The unheroic Canadian self-image (see our Hip features): the country of the background, the almost, the quiet — and the song that made that background feel sacred.
  1. "I was born in the north Ontario" — wait, the line: "There's a town in north Ontario" — Neil Young, "Helpless" (1970). The unnamed place (see our name-dropping feature) that every Canadian recognizes: the cold, the isolation, the blue frozen rain (see our features).
  1. "All of our working-class heroes / Are on the dole." — wait, the actual: "The working-class heroes are on the dole." — actually: "These working-class heroes are all on the dole." — The Tragically Hip, "Bobcaygeon" (1998). The small-town portrait (see our Hip features) where the constellations and the checkerboard floors hold the same sorrow.
  1. "If the world was ending, you'd come over, right?" — JP Saxe, "If the World Was Ending" (2019). The modern entry (see our Saxe feature): the emergency as the excuse for love — the most Canadian of sentiments (the disaster politely endured, the feeling finally admitted).
  1. "The answer, my friend, is blowin' in the wind." — no, American. Swap: "Rise Up" — The Parachute Club (1983). "Rise up, rise up, oh rise and show your power." The dance-floor as politics (see our Parachute Club feature): joy as resistance, 1983 Toronto rendered as promise (see our features).
  1. "Sundown, you better take care / If I find you been creeping 'round my back stairs." — Gordon Lightfoot (1974). The menace beneath the placid (see our Lightfoot feature): the Canadian surface and the dark water under it. The country's double nature in one couplet.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most Canadian lyric ever written?

Consensus candidates: Vigneault's "mon pays... c'est l'hiver," Mitchell's "a case of you," and the Weakerthans' "I hate Winnipeg" (see our features).

Why do Canadian lyrics reference so much weather?

The weather is the emotional landscape (see our craft feature): specific, inescapable, and true — the method behind the national songbook.
