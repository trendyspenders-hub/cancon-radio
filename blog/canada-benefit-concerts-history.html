Canada's Role in History's Biggest Benefit Concerts

When the world's musicians gather for a cause, Canada keeps showing up — and not just with attendees. The country's benefit-concert tradition, from the all-star charity singles of the 80s to the Live 8 era to the streaming-age telethons, has produced some of the most moving mass-music moments in its history. The tradition, on a timeline:

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA TIMELINE

The timeline

1985"Tears Are Not Enough"

Canada's answer to "We Are the World" — and a genuinely great one: dozens of the country's biggest stars (Lightfoot, Neil Young, Joni Mitchell, the Guess Who alumni, Anne Murray, and the 80s wave) assembled in Toronto to record "Tears Are Not Enough" for African famine relief. The song topped the Canadian charts, and the session itself — the country's entire music community in one room, trading verses — became a template for every Canadian all-star gathering that followed.

The concert years — Live Aid's shadow and the Canadian shows

The global benefit-concert era had its Canadian dimension: while Live Aid itself played London and Philadelphia, Canada's industry developed its own benefit apparatus — telethons, all-star shows, and the charity-single infrastructure that kept the country's stars practiced at sharing a stage for a cause.

2005Live 8, Barrie

When the Live 8 organizers planned the 2005 G8-concurrent concerts, Canada got a show of its own: tens of thousands descended on Barrie, Ontario, for the Canadian Live 8 — a marquee lineup before a crowd that dwarfed the town? — keep it clean: a marquee lineup before an enormous crowd, Canada's seat at the biggest benefit-concert table in history. The day had its debates (gated access versus the open ethos?) — keep it clean: the day sparked the era's debates about access and activism — but the image held: Canada, gathered, for the cause.

2010Young Artists for Haiti

After the Haitian earthquake, dozens of Canadian stars — from established legends to the then-rising Drake and Bieber — assembled to record K'naan's "Wavin' Flag" (see our features on both). The result was one of the most moving all-Canadian ensemble recordings ever made: the country's full musical spectrum, from Cohen's lineage to the new Toronto wave, on a single track — and a template for the modern charity single.

The streaming era — telethons and the new togetherness

The benefit format keeps evolving: televised telethons, streaming-era benefit streams, and the pandemic-era variants that replaced the concert with the video call — artists performing from home for a cause. The technology changes; the Canadian instinct doesn't: this is the country that turns gathering into an art form, and the benefit concert is where the art form does its most serious work.

Why the tradition matters

Benefit concerts test a music community's actual community-ness, and Canada's record is strong — because the country's industry is small enough that the all-star session is a family reunion, and communal enough that the reunion usually sings well. "Tears Are Not Enough" to Young Artists for Haiti: forty years of proof that when the cause is right, the country can harmonize on command. That's not a skill every music nation has. It might be the one that matters most.

Frequently asked questions

What was Canada’s “We Are the World”?

“Tears Are Not Enough” (1985) — the all-star Canadian famine-relief single featuring dozens of the country’s biggest names.

Did Canada host a Live 8 concert?

Yes — Live 8’s Canadian show took place in Barrie, Ontario, in 2005, drawing an enormous crowd for the G8-era anti-poverty event.

What is Young Artists for Haiti?

The 2010 charity single — dozens of Canadian stars covering K’naan’s “Wavin’ Flag” for Haitian earthquake relief (see our features).
