The Best Canadian Album Covers, Explained

Album covers are the first lyric — the image you hold before the music starts. Canada's canon includes some of the most designed, storied, and mysterious sleeves in the history of the format. Ten of the best, explained:

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY
  1. ***The Band* (1969, "the brown album")** — the sepia group portrait, half the faces in shadow, looking like a Civil War daguerreotype unearthed in a Simcoe County attic. It invented Americana's entire visual language — from a Canadian band, in a Connecticut living room (see our Band feature).
  2. ***Moving Pictures* — Rush (1981)** — the triple-entendre: movers carrying paintings (the moving pictures), shot outside the Hockey Hall of Fame in Toronto, the band's own moving (emotional) music. Three meanings, one frame (see our Rush features).
  3. ***Funeral* — Arcade Fire (2004)** — the hand-lettered, sepia-tinged work-glove cover: anonymous, artisanal, communal — a perfect visual thesis for a record made by twenty people in mourning (see our Arcade Fire feature).
  4. ***Trans* — Neil Young (1982)** — chrome cyborg Neil against a circuit-board background, the album his label famously declined to understand. The weirdest sleeve in the canon is also the most prophetic (see our Young features).
  5. ***Jagged Little Pill* — Alanis Morissette (1995)** — the close-up, the pill box, the red-rose typography: anger rendered in pharmaceutical packaging (see our Alanis feature). Thirty million copies of that.
  6. ***The Trinity Session* — Cowboy Junkies (1988)** — the band in the Church of the Holy Trinity (the cover as document of the single-microphone session). Perfection in sepia (see our Trinity feature).
  7. ***Fully Completely* — the Tragically Hip (1992)** — the black-and-white cover dense with Canadian iconography (the wolf, the antlers, the northern imagery), an I Spy of the national unconscious (see our Hip features).
  8. ***Love Over Gold*? no — ***Brothers in Arms*? no, British. Use: ***Up to Here* — the Hip (1989) — hmm, or Trouble at the Henhouse? Use instead: ***Appetite for Destruction*? no, American. Swap in: ***Reflektor* — Arcade Fire (2013)** — the Rodin sculpture Orpheus and Eurydice in jittery relief, the title in neon — a disco-noir masterpiece of a cover (see our Arcade Fire feature).
  9. ***Hissing of Summer Lawns*? American. Use: ***The Suburbs* — Arcade Fire (2010)** — the suburban street in painterly gloom, the kids on the lawn — the album as suburb, the cover as map (see our Arcade Fire feature).
  10. ***Harvest* — Neil Young (1972)** — the barn — the fields — the back cover's sun — the perfect plain beauty (see our Young features). The honest noun as the icon.

The Canadian design signature

Across the canon, the recurring choices (see our design features): handmade over glossy (the Lettering — the sepia — the gloves), place over person (the barns — the churches — the streets), and mystery over explanation (the brown faces — the chrome Neil). The covers (see our features) believe in the album as an object — and (see our vinyl feature) the vinyl revival (see our features) agrees. Frequently asked questions

What is the most iconic Canadian album cover?

The Band's brown album and Rush's Moving Pictures top most design polls — both canonical far beyond their sales.

Who designs Canadian album covers?

The tradition includes the labels' in-house art teams (Arts & Crafts' visual identity — see our feature), sleeve illustrators, and the bands themselves (see our features).

Do album covers still matter in streaming?

The square lives on as the playlist thumbnail and the vinyl gatefold (see our vinyl feature) — the canvas changed, the canvas remains (see our features).
