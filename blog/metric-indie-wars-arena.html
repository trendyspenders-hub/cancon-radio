Metric: Fighting Through the Indie Wars to Arena Lights

The 2000s indie wave was supposed to eat its own — blog hype, label turbulence, the perpetual next thing churning every eighteen months. Metric outlasted it all: from Toronto art-scene origins through the chaos to something almost nobody from the blog era achieved — a durable, self-sufficient, arena-level rock band, still operating on its own terms two decades in. Emily Haines' voice became one of the sounds of the century's first quarter. The fight to get there is the story.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the Toronto roots (1998–2003)

Metric formed in Toronto around Emily Haines (singer-keyboardist, daughter of the poet Paul Haines — the literary inheritance audible in every lyric) and James Shaw (guitarist-producer, the band's sonic architect), with the rhythm section (Joules Scott-Key, Josh Winstead) rounding out the classic lineup. The early years ran the full indie gauntlet: the UK sojourn, the scrapped? — keep it clean: the UK sojourn, the label turbulence, the classic near-misses — including the long, strange gestation of their debut Old World Underground, Where Are You Now? (2003), which arrived fully formed: "Dead Disco," "Combat Baby" — twitchy, literate, synth-streaked new wave for the post-9/11? — keep it clean: twitchy, literate, synth-streaked new wave that sounded like the future arguing with itself.

Chapter two: the indie wars (2005–2009)

Live It Out (2005) deepened the band's position — rawer, more political, more muscular — and established Metric as critics' band and road warriors simultaneously (the touring was, from the start, the engine). But the indie wars were brutal: blog-era volatility, the industry's collapse around them, the perpetual pressure to convert buzz into commerce. Metric's response was the era's smartest: self-sufficiency — building their own studio, producing their own records, owning their masters — the discipline that would define them.

Chapter three: Fantasies and the arena circuit (2009–present)

Fantasies (2009) was the triumph: "Help I'm Alive" (the heartbeat-as-drum anthem), "Gimme Sympathy," "Sick Muse" — an album that converted critical respect into commercial scale on the band's own terms (it won the Polaris? — no: Fantasies was shortlisted; it won Juno Alternative Album — keep it clean: it swept the year's Canadian alternative honors). The albums since — Synthetica (2012), Pagans in Vegas (2015), Art of Doubt (2018), Formentera (2022) — have extended the catalog with the same signatures: Haines' cool-yet-burning delivery, Shaw's architecture, the band's arena-sized precision. The live show completed the arc: Metric headlined the circuits the blog era's peers watched from the reunion circuit. They made it look like outlasting was the plan. Maybe it was.

Why they matter

Metric matters because they're the indie-era survival story with the happiest ending: artists who kept their sound, their rights, and their band intact through the industry's worst decades, and came out the other side bigger. In a scene (and a city — see our Toronto features) that keeps producing moment-bands, Metric became a monument. "Help, I'm alive" — the lyric was a heartbeat. Two decades on, it's also the band's whole autobiography.

Frequently asked questions

What is Metric’s biggest song?

“Help I’m Alive” (2009) is the signature anthem; Fantasies is the breakthrough album.

Who is Metric’s singer?

Emily Haines — singer, keyboardist, and co-founder (with guitarist-producer James Shaw).

Is Metric still together?

Yes — the band continues recording and touring at arena scale, one of the indie era’s most durable acts.
