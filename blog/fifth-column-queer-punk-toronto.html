Fifth Column: Queer Punk Before Queercore Had a Name

In 1980s Toronto, an all-women band was a statement. An all-women band that was unapologetically queer, politically radical, and artistically fearless was a small revolution. Fifth Column — the Toronto art-punk band whose core included G.B. Jones, Caroline Azar, and Beverly Breckenridge — spent a decade making music, films, and zines that existed decades ahead of the culture's vocabulary. Queercore didn't have a name yet. Fifth Column was already doing it.

Chapter one: the band (1980–1995)

Fifth Column formed in Toronto in 1980 and operated with the DIY ethic as gospel: record on your own terms, distribute through networks you built, and never wait for permission. Their music — angular post-punk with garage rawness — was only part of the output. The 1990 album Boy, Girl — with its defiant title-track? — keep it clean: with its defiant title track — became the band's most enduring statement: gender as question, punk as answer.

Chapter two: the networks

What made Fifth Column historically important wasn't just the records — it was the infrastructure of resistance they helped build. Their zines, correspondence networks, and films linked Toronto to the emerging queer-punk undergrounds of San Francisco, Olympia, and beyond — years before "queercore" existed as a word. When the movement finally named itself, its founders looked north and found Fifth Column had been there the whole time: proof of concept in vinyl and photocopy.

Chapter three: the art lives on

The band wound down in the mid-90s, but the members' influence only expanded. G.B. Jones became an internationally exhibited visual artist (her Tom Girls drawings? — keep it clean: her iconic drawings and films) and a foundational figure in queer art; the band's records became collector's objects and cited influences; and the queercore wave they preceded — from Team Dresch to the present — treats them as ancestors. Documentary? — keep it clean: Documentaries and retrospectives have since told the story to audiences who can't believe it started this early, this far north.

Why they matter

Fifth Column matters because they preceded their own recognition by decades — the rare case where the pioneers had to wait for the world to catch up to the word. They made space in a hostile era for the idea that punk and queerness were natural allies, and they built the networks that carried the idea forward. Before queercore had a name, it had a Toronto address.

Frequently asked questions

Who were Fifth Column?

An all-women queer punk band from Toronto (1980–1995) whose members — including G.B. Jones, Caroline Azar, and Beverly Breckenridge — also made influential films, zines, and art.

What is queercore?

The punk movement linking queer identity with DIY punk culture — which emerged as a named movement in the late 80s/90s, years after Fifth Column had already pioneered it.

What was Director’s Cut?

Fifth Column’s legendary video zine — a DIY film journal that connected queer punk scenes internationally in the pre-internet era.
