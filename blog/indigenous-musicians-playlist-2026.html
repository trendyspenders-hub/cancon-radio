Indigenous Musicians You Need on Your Playlist in 2026

The most exciting growth story in Canadian music isn't a genre — it's a generation. Indigenous artists across the country are making records that are simultaneously rooted and future-facing, winning Polaris Prizes and JUNOs, and building audiences that span nations and languages. If your playlist doesn't include these ten artists, it has a hole in it.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY
  1. Elisapie — The Inuk artist's Inuktitut (2023), reimagining classic songs in her language, earned the widest international attention of her career and made her a global voice for the North.
  2. A Tribe Called Red (now The Halluci Nation) — The Ottawa group's powwow-step didn't just create a genre; it created a cultural engine — DJ sets, activism, and collaborations that put Indigenous electronic music on the world stage.
  3. Snotty Nose Rez Kids — The Haisla duo's trap is fierce, funny, and politically unflinching — the rowdiest ambassadors Indigenous hip-hop has ever produced.
  4. Tanya Tagaq — Inuk throat singing as avant-garde performance art; her Polaris-winning Animism remains one of the most radical Canadian records of the century.
  5. William Prince — The Peguis-raised songwriter's Gospel First Nation and Stand in the Joy brought Indigenous storytelling to the Americana mainstream with a voice like a wide prairie sky.
  6. The Jerry Cans — Iqaluit's country-folk party band, singing in Inuktitut and making the North dance — proof that “northern music” is a genre with a capital.
  7. Aysanabee — The Oji-Cree singer-songwriter's Here and Now earned Polaris and Juno attention; his voice does things to a room that people describe afterward in lowered voices.
  8. Silla and Rise — Inuit throat singers meet electronic production in a collaboration that feels both ancient and downloaded from the future.
  9. DJ Shub — The Halluci Nation co-founder's solo work keeps the powwow-step flame burning with some of the hardest-hitting beats in the country.
  10. DiggingRoots — Raven Kanatakta and Shoshona Kish's duo blends blues, folk, and rock with Two-Spirit perspective and some of the warmest harmonies in Canadian roots music.

Why this moment matters

For most of Canadian music history, Indigenous artists were excluded from the industry's machinery — from radio to award shows to venues. What's changed isn't talent (that was always there, from Buffy Sainte-Marie and Willie Dunn forward); it's access. Festivals book Indigenous headliners, labels are signing across the North, and Polaris voters keep rewarding the work — five of the last decade's shortlists have featured Indigenous-led records. The result: a generation making music with no need to translate itself for anyone.

How to go deeper

Start with the playlists (the artists' own picks are always the best), then the archives: Buffy Sainte-Marie's 60s folk-fury, Willie Dunn's protest records, the RPM (Revolutions Per Minute) collections of Indigenous music. Then catch these artists live — the powwow-step sets especially, where the tradition's oldest rhythm meets its newest technology, and the future of Canadian music is dancing in front of you.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the biggest Indigenous Canadian musicians right now?

Tanya Tagaq, A Tribe Called Red/The Halluci Nation, Elisapie, William Prince, and Snotty Nose Rez Kids lead the current wave, with Aysanabee among the fastest-rising.

What is powwow-step?

The genre A Tribe Called Red created — electronic dance music built on powwow drum and vocal samples, blending Indigenous tradition with club production.

Where should I start with Indigenous Canadian music?

Elisapie's Inuktitut, Tagaq's Animism, and The Halluci Nation's We Are the Halluci Nation are the modern cornerstones; Buffy Sainte-Marie is the essential historical starting point.
