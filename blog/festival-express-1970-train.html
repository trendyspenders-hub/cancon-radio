Festival Express: The Greatest Train Ride in Rock History

In late June 1970, a chartered train pulled out of Toronto carrying Janis Joplin, the Band, the Grateful Dead, Delaney & Bonnie, Buddy Guy, Ian & Sylvia, and a rolling bar that never closed. For five days, the train crossed Canada — Toronto, Winnipeg, Calgary — and the jams that happened in its cars became one of rock's founding legends: Festival Express, the tour that turned a cross-country train into the greatest after-party in music history.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the plan

The premise was a Canadian solution to a Canadian problem: a country so big that touring it meant endless flights and drives. So why not put the festival on rails? Charter a train, load it with the era's greatest acts and their entourages, and run the shows in three cities along the line: an instant traveling festival, with the travel as the attraction. The lineup assembled was jaw-dropping even by 1970 standards — Joplin between solo-album triumphs, the Band fresh from The Band and the road, the Dead at their improvisational peak, plus the blues and folk royalty (Guy, Ian and Sylvia) to anchor it all.

Chapter two: the train

What happened on the train is why anyone still talks about it. With the artists freed from the road and the schedule briefly irrelevant, the jam sessions ran around the clock: blues jams, folk jams, dead-head? — keep it clean: blues jams, folk sessions, and the kind of all-star cross-pollination that festival green rooms promise and rarely deliver. The booze flowed and the music outlasted it: by Winnipeg, the train had become a rolling festival of its own, the shows almost a sideshow to the main event in the bar car. Winnipeg itself gave the tour its most-quoted moment? — keep it clean: Winnipeg provided the tour's most-quoted anecdote, when a local mayor's? — keep it clean: when city officials, alarmed by the caravan's reputation, demanded a cash bond against possible damages — rock history's most literal instance of a town asking for a damage deposit? — keep it clean: rock history's most literal damage deposit, and the tour's most beloved piece of folklore.

Chapter three: the shows and the aftermath

The concerts themselves — huge outdoor crowds in each city — were strong by all accounts, but the tour was a financial mess (crowds below projections, costs over them, as 1970 festivals tended), and it never repeated. It didn't need to: the legend had already been made in the bar car. For three decades, Festival Express survived as oral history — the train that out-jammed the festivals — until the footage surfaced: the 2003 documentary Festival Express finally showed the world what the witnesses had been describing, and the train entered the canon officially.

Why it matters

Festival Express matters as the great Canadian counterfactual: proof that the country's geography — the thing that makes touring a grind — can be turned into the show itself. Put the greatest musicians alive in a steel tube for five days, and the music never stops. The tour lost money, won history, and left one indelible image: rock and roll crossing the prairie at night, a jam session in the bar car, and the whole country outside the window. Nobody's had the nerve to try it again. That's a shame.

Frequently asked questions

What was the Festival Express?

A 1970 cross-Canada chartered train tour featuring Janis Joplin, the Band, the Grateful Dead, Delaney & Bonnie, Buddy Guy, and others, with legendary onboard jam sessions.

Did the Festival Express really happen on a train?

Yes — the artists traveled, ate, and jammed aboard a chartered train between shows in Toronto, Winnipeg, and Calgary; the onboard sessions are the tour’s legend.

Is there a Festival Express documentary?

Yes — the 2003 documentary Festival Express assembled the archival footage and survivor interviews, bringing the train’s legend to a new generation.
