Purity Ring: Shrines and the Glowing Pop Sound

In 2012, a duo from Edmonton — of all places — released a debut that redefined what Canadian electronic pop could feel like: Shrines by Purity Ring, a record of dark, glowing, bodily? — keep it clean: dark, glowing, hyper-detailed pop that sounded like nothing else on earth — and whose sound (Megan James' floating vocals over Corin Roddick's precision-tooled production) became a template for a decade of electronic pop. The glowing sound had a source. It was the prairies.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: Edmonton to the internet

James and Roddick came up through Edmonton's all-ages punk and electronic scenes (see our prairie features) before relocating — the classic prairie-artist trajectory — and building Purity Ring's sound in isolation: Roddick's production (custom-built? — keep it light: meticulously crafted, organic-mechanical beats) under James' lyrics, which treated the body as landscape and nature as architecture — imagery that gave the music its uncanny warmth. The early tracks ("Ungirthed," "Belispeak") spread through the blog ecosystem (see our blog-house feature) with the velocity of genuine newness; 4AD signed them; the debut arrived with impossible expectations. It exceeded them.

Chapter two: Shrines and the glow

Shrines (2012) delivered the thesis: "Fineshrine" — the signature single, and one of the decade's most distinctive pop songs — plus "Amenamy," "Obedear," "Grandloves": a record that fused the intimacy of bedroom production with the sweep of arena electronica, the dark-and-glowing duality that became the genre's default mode. Critics canonized it; the touring rig (Roddick's homemade? — keep it light: Roddick's custom lighting-and-production rig, a genuine innovation in live electronic performance) made the concerts events; and the influence compounded — the "glowing" strain of 2010s electronic pop (the sound beneath a generation of producers) traces back to Shrines with unusual directness.

Chapter three: the slow continuation

Another Eternity (2015) polished the formula for bigger rooms; Womb (2020) — arriving after a long gestation — returned to the hushed intimacy of the debut's best moments. The duo's pace has always been deliberate (see our slow-songs feature): Purity Ring records are built, and the building takes the time it takes. A decade-plus in, the glow they invented is everywhere — the sound of modern electronic pop's warmest corners — and the duo endures as its source: quiet, precise, and still glowing.

Why they matter

They matter because Shrines is one of those debuts that re-tunes the genre's ear — the record after which electronic pop's producers reached for warmth as well as weight. The prairies keep producing the unexpected (see our prairie features). Purity Ring produced a sound the whole world borrowed.

Frequently asked questions

What is Purity Ring’s best album?

Shrines (2012) is the landmark debut — “Fineshrine” its signature song.

Where is Purity Ring from?

Edmonton, Alberta — the duo (Megan James and Corin Roddick) built their sound in prairie isolation before relocating.

What did Purity Ring influence?

The “glowing” strain of 2010s electronic pop — the warm, dark, hyper-detailed sound that became a decade-wide default.
