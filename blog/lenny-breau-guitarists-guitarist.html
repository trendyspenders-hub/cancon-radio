Lenny Breau: The Guitarist's Guitarist

Ask the world's greatest guitarists about Lenny Breau and watch them go quiet. Chet Atkins called him the best guitarist in the world and signed? — keep it clean: and championed him; his admirers form a who's-who of the instrument; and yet Breau died in 1984, essentially broke and obscure, his genius preserved on a handful of recordings and in the muscle memory of every fingerstyle player who followed. The Maine-born, Canada-raised guitarist didn't just master his instrument — he reimagined what it could do.

Chapter one: the Breau family

Breau was born in Auburn, Maine, in 1941, into a family of professional country musicians — his parents, "Lone Pine" and Betty Cody, were a working Maine country act, and Lenny grew up on the family circuit, a child prodigy playing lead guitar in the family band from toddlerhood? — keep it clean: from early childhood. The family relocated to Maritime Canada — the Breau Family became a Canadian country institution, and young Lenny soaked up everything: country picking, jazz records, classical ideas. By his teens, the country circuit was too small for what he was hearing.

Chapter two: the Winnipeg discovery

The pivotal chapter came in Winnipeg, where Breau settled in the early 60s and became a legend in the city's jazz scene — a guitarist doing things with fingerstyle technique that players simply didn't do: comping chords, melody, and walking bass lines simultaneously, with a tone and touch that sounded like several guitarists at once. A local TV performance reached Chet Atkins, who tracked Breau down and became his patron — recording him, championing him, and announcing to the guitar world that a new genius had arrived. The Atkins-produced The Velvet Touch of Lenny Breau — Live! (1969) introduced the marvel: jazz harmony, country fluency, and a seven-string guitar (adding a high A string) that extended his already impossible reach.

Chapter three: the struggle

Breau's career, like too many genius stories, is also a tragedy of systems. He was a perfectionist who rarely finished records, a gentle man ill-suited to the business, and a restless artist whose interests (flamenco, Indian classical music, the avant-garde) scattered his output. He recorded the revered Live at the Village Vanguard (1982) — finally, a document worthy of the legend — but by then, decades of substance struggles and industry neglect had taken their toll. In 1984, he was found dead in a Los Angeles swimming pool, at 43, under circumstances still unresolved. The guitar world mourned — and then, belatedly, canonized.

Chapter four: the afterlife of influence

Posthumously, Breau has become exactly what he should have been in life: a foundational figure. The **documentary *The Genius of Lenny Breau*** spread his story; reissues made his catalog accessible; and a generation of fingerstyle players — from jazz artists to New Acoustic Music? — keep it clean: from jazz guitarists to acoustic virtuosos — treats his technique as essential curriculum. The tragedy isn't just that he died young. It's that the world was so slow to make room for what he was. The music knew all along.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Lenny Breau?

A Maine-born, Canada-raised jazz guitarist — raised in a country-music family — who pioneered modern fingerstyle technique and is revered by guitarists worldwide.

What did Chet Atkins say about Lenny Breau?

Atkins championed Breau as one of the greatest guitarists alive and recorded him in 1969 — the endorsement that introduced Breau to the wider guitar world.

How did Lenny Breau die?

He died in 1984 at age 43, found in a Los Angeles swimming pool under unresolved circumstances — a loss the guitar world still mourns.
