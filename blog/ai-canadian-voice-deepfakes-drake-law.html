AI and the Canadian Voice: The Week an AI Broke the Music Industry — From Toronto

In April 2023, a mysterious song called "Heart on My Sleeve" appeared on streaming platforms: a convincingly Drake-like voice trading verses with an equally convincing fake The Weeknd. Nobody knew who made it. Everybody played it. Within days it was a genuine chart contender, and the music industry — Canadian artists and their lawyers very much included — was forced to confront a question it had postponed for years: who owns a voice?

BY CANCON RADIO4 MIN READTHE CASE FOR AND AGAINST

The case for alarm: this is theft at the speed of software

"Heart on My Sleeve" wasn't a demo — it was a heist

The anonymous creator, "Ghostwriter," hadn't remixed anything; they'd synthesized performances indistinguishable from two of the world's biggest artists without consent, compensation, or credit. Universal Music Group's takedown demands framed it plainly: training AI on an artist's voice without permission is unauthorized use of their identity and catalog. From this side, the stakes are existential: if a convincing fake can be generated in a bedroom, the value of the real thing — the years, the craft, the catalog — collapses toward zero.

Drake's own brush proved the point

In 2024, Drake released "Taylor Made Freestyle" — AI-generated verses in the voices of Tupac Shakur and Snoop Dogg aimed at Kendrick Lamar. Even an artist of Drake's stature, using the tech against rivals, had to pull the track within days after Tupac's estate threatened legal action. If the world's most-streamed artist can't deploy a deepfake without consequences, the technology's legal reality is clear: voices are property now, or soon will be.

The law is scrambling — and Canada is in the middle of it

Canada's existing toolkit — copyright, moral rights, passing-off, and personality rights that vary by province — was built for an era when copying a voice required actually hiring the singer. New federal AI legislation and private-member bills on digital replicas have raced to catch up, while the CRTC's streaming-era mandate tries to answer an adjacent question: if the algorithm can't tell Drake from a fake, what does "Canadian content" even mean? The regulatory fight is Canadian whether Canadians like it or not, because the two most famous early victims were Torontonians.

The case for calm: every panicked industry said the same thing

Sampling was supposed to end music, too

When samplers arrived, the industry predicted catastrophe; what followed was hip-hop — a whole new golden age built on exactly the technology feared most, eventually regulated rather than banned. The synthesizer was going to replace orchestras; instead it created new genres. The historical pattern is unbroken: new sound-making tools produce panic, then law, then art. AI vocals may be no different — a crude phase now, a legitimate instrument later, with consent frameworks settling the ethics.

The "Heart on My Sleeve" silver lining

Strip the theft away and something remarkable remains: millions of listeners were moved by a song no human superstar sang. The creative hunger it revealed — for new music in familiar voices — is real, and it isn't going away. Artists are already using voice models with consent: estates licensing digital resurrections, singers training models on their own voices to extend their range, producers using AI as a sketching tool the way they once used drum machines. The tool isn't the villain; the consent is the whole question.

The market punishes fakes — eventually

Audiences turned on the fake Drake single almost as fast as they embraced it once the novelty curdled into unease. Fakes without a human behind them lack the one thing fandom actually trades in: a person. The enduring value of a voice, this argument runs, is precisely that it can't be counterfeited — because what we're buying is the biography attached to it.

The verdict: consent is the whole ballgame

Both cases agree on one thing, which is why this argument is really a negotiation. Nobody serious wants to ban the technology; everybody serious wants the consent question settled. The likeliest future looks like sampling's: a licensing regime where voices are cleared, compensated, and credited — where "featuring the estate of" becomes as normal as "featuring" did. Until then, Canada sits at the center of the storm it unwittingly started, and the fake Drake on your playlist isn't just a curiosity. It's a first draft of the next fifty years of music law.

Frequently asked questions

What was "Heart on My Sleeve"?

An anonymous 2023 track using AI-generated vocals imitating Drake and The Weeknd; it went viral before being pulled from platforms after label objections.

Did Drake use AI in his own music?

Yes — the 2024 track "Taylor Made Freestyle" used AI voices of Tupac Shakur and Snoop Dogg; it was withdrawn after legal threats from Tupac's estate.

Is voice cloning illegal in Canada?

There is no single law yet covering AI voice replicas; cases proceed through copyright, personality rights, and passing-off law, while new federal legislation has been proposed. The legal framework is actively being built — largely because of Canadian artists.
