Godspeed You! Black Emperor: The Sound of Montreal's Apocalypse

Godspeed You! Black Emperor — the Montreal collective whose slow-burning, apocalyptic instrumentals became the defining sound of a whole strain of underground music — made Canadian indie unmarketable on purpose and became globally revered for it. Their career is a map of everything the country's independent music can do when it refuses to be consumed.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the origin (1994–1997)

Godspeed formed in Montreal in 1994 — a loose collective of musicians, film students, and activists — and began performing long, improvised pieces in loft spaces and small venues. The 1997 debut F A ∞ (see our Constellation feature) arrived like a prophecy: side-length movements, found-sound fragments (train whistles, street preachers, the drone of machines), and the sense that the world was ending and the band was scoring it. The record — released on Constellation, the label the band co-founded — established the template: no lyrics, no frontman, no interviews, the music as a communal, almost ritual experience. The album became a word-of-mouth legend; the shows became events — hours-long, immersive, the band silhouetted against film projections, the audience sitting on the floor.

Chapter two: the imperial era (2000–2010)

Lift Your Skinny Fists Like Antennas to Heaven (2000) — the double-album epic that most critics rank as the masterpiece — expanded the canvas: two discs, four movements, the band's emotional and musical range stretched to symphonic breadth. The Yanqui U.X.O. (2002) era followed; the hiatus (2003–2010) — the band's members scattering to other projects (Thee Silver Mt. Zion, Set Fire to Flames) — only deepened the legend. The reunion (2010–2012) produced 'Allelujah! Don't Bend! Ascend! (2012), which won the Polaris Music Prize (see our Polaris feature) — announced, characteristically, with a statement critiquing the music industry itself. The award was the underground's ultimate vindication: the anti-industry band, crowned by the industry's most prestigious prize, using the platform to denounce the prize's premise.

Chapter three: the uncompromising present

The 2010s and beyond — Asunder, Sweet and Other Distress (2015), Luciferian Towers (2017), G_d's Pee AT STATE'S END! (2021) — have found Godspeed continuing at their own pace, unhurried, uncommercial, the shows still events (the projections, the volume, the communal silence between the crescendos), the records still arguments (against capitalism, against complacency, against the end of the world they're scoring). The band remains a working collective — members rotating, the mission constant — the Montreal apocalypse still burning, the rest of the world still catching up.

Why they matter

They matter because they proved that the most uncompromising art can find its audience — the long, wordless, political instrumental, the show as ritual, the band as collective — and that Canada's independent music can sustain world-historical ambition without a single radio spin or industry compromise. The apocalypse they scored turned out to be the one we got; the music still helps.

Frequently asked questions

Who are Godspeed You! Black Emperor?

A Montreal instrumental collective (formed 1994) — post-rock pioneers known for long, cinematic compositions and uncompromising DIY ethics.

What is Godspeed’s best album?

Lift Your Skinny Fists Like Antennas to Heaven (2000) is the consensus masterpiece; F A ∞ (1997) is the landmark debut.

Did Godspeed win the Polaris Prize?

Yes — 'Allelujah! Don't Bend! Ascend! won the 2013 Polaris, announced with a characteristically critical statement about the music business.
