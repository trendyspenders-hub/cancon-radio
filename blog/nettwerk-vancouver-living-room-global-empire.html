Nettwerk: From a Vancouver Living Room to a Global Empire

In 1984, a group of friends in Vancouver started a record company with roughly nothing: no office to speak of, no industry connections worth the name, and a hunch that the music they loved could travel. Four decades later, Nettwerk Music Group is one of the most enduring independent music companies on earth — the label that broke Sarah McLachlan, nurtured Skinny Puppy, and built a global management and publishing empire without ever selling its soul. This is how a Canadian indie did what the majors said couldn't be done.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the living room years (1984–1988)

Nettwerk was founded by Terry McBride, Mark Jowett, Ric Arboit, and Dan Fraser — four young Vancouverites with overlapping jobs at a record store and overlapping taste in what wasn't yet called alternative. The first releases were scrappy and local: a compilation here, a single there, run out of apartments with mailing lists built by hand. The breakthrough came from an unlikely corner: Skinny Puppy, the industrial-electronic pioneers whose confrontational, sample-heavy records found a global audience through import buzz and relentless touring. Nettwerk had proven it could sell the un-sellable — dark, difficult electronic music from Canada to the world — which meant it could sell anything it believed in.

Chapter two: Sarah McLachlan and the art of patience (1988–1997)

The label's defining signing arrived in 1988: a 20-year-old Halifax singer-songwriter named Sarah McLachlan. What followed was a masterclass in independent artist development that the industry still studies: Nettwerk (and McBride as her manager) refused to chase the American single, built McLachlan's audience album by album — Touch, Solace, Fumbling Towards Ecstasy — and let Surfacing (1997) arrive as an event rather than a gamble. Eight million records, multiple Grammys, and the Lilith Fair revolution later, McLachlan's career remains the template for how to develop a Canadian artist for the world without breaking them in the process. Nettwerk's other lanes hummed in parallel: Delerium and the ambient-ethereal project Conjure One turned downtempo electronica into a surprisingly durable global business.

Chapter three: the empire of services (2000s)

While the label arm kept signing (Mother Mother, Stereophonics? — keep it clean: Mother Mother and a string of international signings), Nettwerk quietly built what actually made it an empire: management, publishing, and artist services. Terry McBride became one of the most respected managers in the business; Nettwerk's management roster grew international; and the company pioneered the "360" services model — marketing, digital strategy, sync, and distribution under one roof — years before the majors admitted the album business was over. The lesson of Nettwerk's longevity is unglamorous and profound: own the relationships, serve the artist, and let the formats come and go.

Chapter four: the stubborn independent

Through every industry convulsion — the CD collapse, the download era, streaming — Nettwerk stayed independent, stayed Vancouver-based, and stayed profitable, which in the record business qualifies as a magic trick. McBride's advocacy for artists' rights (he was an early, vocal critic of major-label accounting and a champion of the "emotional copyright" idea? — keep it clean: a vocal advocate for fairer artist deals) made Nettwerk a moral reference point as much as a commercial one. The label's current roster — spanning Canadian indie, international singer-songwriters, and electronic music — keeps the living-room ethos intact: sign what you love, build it patiently, and never confuse the map for the territory.

Why Nettwerk matters

Nettwerk proved that a Canadian independent could compete globally without moving to New York, selling to a major, or compromising its signings. Every indie label in the country — Arts & Crafts, Mint, Royal Mountain — works partly in Nettwerk's shadow. And the McLachlan playbook (artist first, album patience, world later) is, functionally, the national model. It started in a Vancouver living room. It ended up as the architecture.

Frequently asked questions

Who founded Nettwerk?

Terry McBride, Mark Jowett, Ric Arboit, and Dan Fraser, in Vancouver in 1984.

What artists are on Nettwerk?

Sarah McLachlan and Skinny Puppy are the foundational names; Delerium, Mother Mother, and a long international roster followed across the label's decades.

Is Nettwerk still independent?

Yes — it remains independently owned, Vancouver-based, and active across label, management, publishing, and artist services.
