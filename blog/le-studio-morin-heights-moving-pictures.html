Le Studio: The Woodland Studio Behind Moving Pictures

Some studios are rooms with equipment. A few are places — where the geography gets into the tapes. Le Studio, the residential recording complex André Perry built in 1972 in Morin-Heights, Quebec — an hour north of Montreal, deep in the Laurentian hills — was the Canadian example: a chalet on a lake where the world's biggest bands came to live inside their records. Rush made their masterpiece there. The Police made theirs. The tapes still smell like pine.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the vision

André Perry, a young engineer with classical training and an engineer's impatience? — keep it clean: an engineer's restlessness — believed musicians made better records when they weren't watching the clock in a city basement. His model was the residential studio before the concept had a name: a beautiful house on a lake, a world-class control room, and nothing to do between takes but walk in the woods, swim, and argue about music. Artists stayed for weeks, not days. Records became lived-in — a quality you can hear.

Chapter two: the masterpieces

The client list reads like a classic-rock museum wing. Rush made Permanent Waves, Moving Pictures, and Signals at Le Studio — the peak of the band's imperial phase, with the lake and the quiet baked into records that sound enormous and intimate at once. The Police recorded Ghost in the Machine and Synchronicity there — two of the biggest albums of the 80s, born in a Quebec chalet. David Bowie, April Wine, Keith Richards? — keep it clean: Bowie worked there; the guest book stacked legends. Producers and engineers who apprenticed at Le Studio — including a young Bob Rock, who cut his teeth in its rooms — carried its methods (and its phone book) into the rest of Canadian recording history.

Chapter three: the mythology

Le Studio became famous not just for what it produced but for how it felt: the isolation that made bands become families or break apart, the lake frozen under January sessions, the bear that reportedly? — keep it clean: the wildlife stories that every session player swears are true. Rush's affection for the place was so complete that the band kept returning long after they could have recorded anywhere on earth — the strongest endorsement a studio can receive.

Chapter four: the farewell

The studio's later decades were quieter; Perry eventually sold the property, and the building met a tragic end — a fire in 2017 destroyed the structure that had held so much music. The community's response told the real story: tributes from musicians worldwide, and a campaign to preserve the legacy. You can burn a chalet. You can't burn Moving Pictures.

Why it matters

Le Studio proved Canada could host the world's biggest records without apology — not as a cheap satellite room, but as a destination, a place artists chose over London and LA. In the geography of Canadian recording history — Phase One and the Sound of the 80s in Toronto, Little Mountain in Vancouver, Grant Avenue in Hamilton — Le Studio is the retreat where the laurels came to rest.

Frequently asked questions

What albums were recorded at Le Studio?

Rush’s Permanent Waves, Moving Pictures, and Signals; The Police’s Ghost in the Machine and Synchronicity; plus sessions for David Bowie and April Wine, among many others.

Who founded Le Studio?

André Perry, who built the residential studio in Morin-Heights, Quebec, in 1972 — pioneering the “live-in” recording model in Canada.

Does Le Studio still exist?

No — the original building was destroyed by fire in 2017, though its recorded legacy and the memories of the artists who worked there endure.
