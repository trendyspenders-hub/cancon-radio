One-Hit Wonders of Canadian Radio: Where Are They Now?

One-hit wonder is usually a lazy label — most "one-hit" artists had whole careers on either side of the hit. But the Canadian canon is unusually rich in artists who scored one massive, undeniable, world-conquering single and then became trivia questions. We tracked down ten of the best. The afterlives are more interesting than you think.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY
  1. Snow (“Informer,” 1993) — Darrin O'Brien built a genuine international reggae career, particularly in Japan and the Caribbean; the hit funds a life of quiet credibility in the genre he loved before it loved him back.
  2. Len (“Steal My Sunshine,” 1999) — The Costanzo siblings walked away from pop stardom deliberately, writing and producing for other artists; the song now returns every June like a national holiday.
  3. Terry Jacks (“Seasons in the Sun,” 1974) — After the global tearjerker and its follow-up hit, Jacks retreated to the West Coast to produce (notably for Nana Mouskouri), fish, and live an enviably sane life away from the machine.
  4. The Bells (“Stay Awhile,” 1971) — The Toronto family band faded after their AM-gold smash, but the song never left oldies radio; members kept performing for decades in various configurations.
  5. Martha and the Muffins (“Echo Beach,” 1980) — Critical favorites across several albums; Martha Johnson built a respected second career in music education, and the band still performs the song that named a thousand daydreams.
  6. Men Without Hats (“The Safety Dance,” 1983) — Ivan Doroschuk kept the band alive across decades of revivals; “Pop Goes the World” charted too, making them arguably a two-hit act — but who's counting?
  7. Crash Test Dummies (“Mmm Mmm Mmm Mmm,” 1994) — A Juno-winning album and global fame preceded a graceful cult scale; Brad Roberts survived a serious car crash and kept recording the literate, weird catalog.
  8. Daniel Powter (“Bad Day,” 2005) — The soft-rock phenomenon receded from the spotlight but continued writing and releasing music, including well-regarded later records far from the shadow of the world's favorite bad day.
  9. Kiesza (“Hideaway,” 2014) — A dance-pop rocket ship that crash-landed through industry whiplash; she pivoted to independent releases, acting, and a devoted following that knows the second album is the good one.
  10. Trans-X (“Living on Video,” 1983) — A European club phenomenon more than a home one; Pascal Languirand has kept the project alive and the song still fills floors from Paris to Warsaw.

What the label gets wrong

“One-hit wonder” measures radio in one country at one moment. Snow had a platinum album and a Jamaican following; Martha and the Muffins had a decade of critical records; the Bells were stars across the Commonwealth. The hit was the headline, not the whole story — which is why the best of these artists are still working, still touring, and still collecting royalties from a song everyone claims to be tired of and everyone can sing.

The three types of afterlife

Track the data and the one-hitters sort into three buckets: the deliberate exits (Len, Terry Jacks), who chose sanity over stardom; the lifers (Snow, Doroschuk, Trans-X), who kept playing the music they'd have played anyway; and the pivots (Powter, Kiesza), who absorbed the industry's whiplash and rebuilt smaller, weirder, truer. All three are happier endings than the label implies.

Frequently asked questions

What is the biggest one-hit wonder in Canadian history?

By global sales, Terry Jacks' “Seasons in the Sun” (1974) and Snow's “Informer” (1993) lead the discussion; by cultural saturation, “The Safety Dance” has a case.

Is Len still making music?

Marc and Sharon Costanzo stepped back from the spotlight after “Steal My Sunshine” and have worked behind the scenes as writers and producers; the song's seasonal streaming life continues to pay the mortgage.

What happened to Kiesza after “Hideaway”?

After industry turbulence she went independent, continued releasing music, and built a devoted cult audience — her post-hit records are where the real songwriting lives.
