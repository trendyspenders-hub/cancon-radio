Prism: "Spaceship Superstar" and the Vancouver Sound

Before Vancouver was the city of Little Mountain and Bob Rock (see our features), it was the city of Prism — a band that began, fittingly, as a studio project and became one of the late-70s' most successful Canadian rock acts. Prism's story is the West Coast studio era's origin story: players, producers, and songwriters assembling a band the way they'd assemble a record — and accidentally defining a sound.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the studio origins (1975–1977)

Prism coalesced around guitarist Lindsay Mitchell, singer Ron Tabak, and producer Bruce Fairbairn — the future hit-maker (see our Fairbairn feature) — with songwriter Jim Vallance (later Bryan Adams' essential collaborator) supplying material. The self-titled debut (1977) delivered "Spaceship Superstar" — a sci-fi radio-rock gem whose sleek, machine-precise production announced the Vancouver studio sound years before the term existed. Prism was, functionally, what happens when a city's session culture decides to be a band.

Chapter two: the arena breakthrough (1979–1981)

Armageddon (1979) broke Prism wide: the title track and "Virginia"? — keep it clean: the title track and its singles carried the band to platinum-level success at home and real American traction — the sleek hard-rock sound (Fairbairn's precision production, Mitchell's guitar, Tabak's keening vocals) filling arenas across the West. The follow-ups kept the streak: "Young and Restless," "Night to Remember," and the ballad breakthrough "Don't Let Him Know" (1982), which gave Prism genuine US top-40 success — the studio band's craft paying off at pop scale.

Chapter three: the legacy of the machine

Prism's later years brought the classic rock-band turbulence (vocalist changes, the eternal drift), but the band's real legacy is structural: it was the template for the Vancouver sound that followed — the Fairbairn production dynasty (Prism → Loverboy → the Payolas → AC/DC and Aerosmith), the Vallance songwriting axis (Prism → Adams), the whole idea of the West Coast studio as the band's secret weapon. Prism endures as a beloved arena-rock catalog ("Spaceship Superstar" remains a CanCon staple) and as the proof that Vancouver's great rock era began, appropriately, in a control room.

Why they matter

Prism matters because it came first: before the arenas, before the export hits, there was a Vancouver studio band making sleek, futuristic rock that sounded like the city's eventual self-image. They were the prototype — the machine that taught the machine how to run. The spaceship launched; the whole West Coast sound was on it.

Frequently asked questions

What was Prism’s biggest hit?

“Spaceship Superstar” (1977) is the signature record; “Don’t Let Him Know” (1982) was their biggest US chart success; Armageddon (1979) was the breakthrough album.

Who produced Prism?

Bruce Fairbairn — the Vancouver producer who went on to shape Loverboy, Aerosmith, and AC/DC (see our Fairbairn feature).

What was the Vancouver sound Prism helped launch?

The sleek, precision-produced West Coast rock that defined Canadian FM in the late 70s and became an international export through the 80s.
