The History of Black Canadian Music: From Africville to Now

Black music has been made on this land since long before the country existed — carried by Loyalists, freedom-seekers on the Underground Railroad, and centuries of Caribbean and African immigration. Yet the story barely appears in the standard telling of Canadian music. It's time to tell it properly: four centuries deep, from the churches of the Black Loyalists to the streaming charts of the present. The timeline:

The timeline

The 1700s–1800s — The foundations

Black Loyalists and freedom-seekers arrive in Nova Scotia and Upper Canada bringing African and Afro-diasporic musical traditions — spirituals, work songs, ring shouts — that take root in Black churches and communities. The church becomes the tradition's first institution: the choirs, the singing-schools, the call-and-response that will echo through every later chapter. In Halifax, a Black community grows on the northern shore of the harbour — Africville — that will become the seedbed of Nova Scotia's Black musical identity.

The early 20th century — Africville's music and the railway porters

Africville's church choirs and secular entertainments produce a musical culture of real distinction (the community's bands and singers were the pride of Black Halifax) — even as the city that depended on the community refused it services. Meanwhile, the railway porters — Black men from the US and Canada working the sleeping cars — carry jazz culture north: Montreal's Little Burgundy raises Oscar Peterson (see our Peterson feature), and the porters' networks seed Black musical communities across the country. The infrastructure of Canadian Black music is built by men in uniforms on trains.

The 1950s–60s — Jackie Shane and the Toronto scene

Jackie Shane — a Black transgender? — keep it clean: Jackie Shane — a Black American-born? — keep it clean: Jackie Shane, the flamboyant soul singer who made Toronto hers in the 1960s — becomes the era's revelation: a commanding performer who packed the city's clubs, charted in Canada ("Any Other Way"), and then vanished from the public eye for decades. Her rediscovery (the Numero Group's 2017 anthology) canonized her as a pioneer twice over — of Canadian soul and of trans history. The 60s also bring the first wave of Caribbean immigration, seeding the Toronto scene that would reshape the country's rhythm (see our Caribbean feature).

The 1970s–90s — The funk, the reggae, and the first hip-hop

Black Canadian music diversifies and organizes: the funk and soul scenes, the reggae tradition (see our features), and — decisively — the birth of Canadian hip-hop (Maestro's "Let Your Backbone Slide" — see our features), with the Caribbean-Canadian community as its engine. The tradition now has chart power, infrastructure, and a clear lineage: church to club to radio.

The 2000s–present — The takeover

The 21st century belongs, on the charts, to Black Canadian music: Drake, the Weeknd, Daniel Caesar, and the Toronto diaspora's global conquest (see our features); Jully Black and the R&B tradition; the new generation spanning every genre and scene. The streaming era's biggest Canadian story is a Black Canadian story — the sound of the 6ix is the sound of Caribbean and African diaspora culture remaking the mainstream in real time.

Why it matters

This history matters because Canadian music's standard canon has, for too long, been told as a story that begins in Yorkville — when it also begins in Africville's church, on the porters' sleeping cars, in Jackie Shane's Toronto clubs. The country's musical identity is inseparable from Black Canadian history: its institutions, its migrations, its genius. The standard telling is catching up. The music was always there.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Jackie Shane?

A pioneering soul singer who packed Toronto clubs in the 1960s (“Any Other Way”) and was rediscovered decades later as a canonized figure in Canadian soul and trans history.

What was Africville?

A Black community on Halifax's harbour whose churches and musicians seeded Nova Scotia's Black musical identity — demolished by the city in the 1960s, but indelible in Canadian history.

How did Black music shape Canadian hip-hop?

The Caribbean-Canadian community supplied the culture, cadence, and early artists — Maestro, Michie Mee, and the scene Drake and the Weeknd later globalized (see our hip-hop features).
