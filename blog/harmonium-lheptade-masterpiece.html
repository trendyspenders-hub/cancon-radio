Harmonium: L'Heptade's Prog-Folk Masterpiece

In 1976, a Montreal trio walked into a studio and made what many consider the greatest album in Quebec history — a double-LP prog-folk suite, sung entirely in French, that filled the province's arenas and meant essentially nothing to the English-speaking world. L'Heptade by Harmonium is the Canadian canon its own country keeps misplacing: a masterpiece the size of The Wall, as beloved in Quebec as Blue is anywhere, and overdue for the rest of the country's attention.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the rise (1972–1975)

Harmonium formed around Serge Fiori and Michel Normandeau — two young Montrealers with luminous harmonies and a folk sensibility sharpened by the era's prog ambitions. Their self-titled debut (1974) gave Quebec the hit "Pour un instant" — a sun-dappled folk-pop song that became an instant standard — and Les cinq saisons (1975) deepened the brew: acoustic textures, poetic French lyrics, and the first sweep of the ambition to come. The province fell hard. Harmonium weren't a band Quebec liked; they were a band Quebec claimed — the chanson tradition (see our feature) graduating to album-scale art.

Chapter two: the masterpiece (1976)

L'Heptade — seven movements, a double album, a suite following a day from dawn to night as a metaphor for a life — was the summit: Fiori's songwriting at its most ambitious, the harmonies stacked into choral walls, the arrangements swelling from acoustic intimacy to full symphonic breadth. The record filled arenas (in Quebec, a francophone prog-folk double album was a commercial phenomenon), earned the province's enduring devotion, and then — because it was French, because Quebec's exports rarely crossed the language border, because the Anglo-Canadian industry barely tried — remained unknown to the rest of the country. The critical consensus in Quebec has never wavered: L'Heptade is the towering achievement of Quebecois album rock, and one of the great Canadian records, full stop.

Chapter three: the short blaze and the long shadow

Harmonium ended almost as soon as they peaked — the classic arc of intense bands — with Fiori moving to a major solo career (including the landmark Deux cents nuits à l'heure with Robert Charlebois). But the shadow of L'Heptade never lifted: the album passed down through Quebec generations as sacred text, its songs woven into the province's emotional fabric, its influence audible in everything from francophone indie to the symphonic ambitions of later Quebec artists. And the rest of the country? It's slowly catching on — the reissue era, the streaming age, and the growing Anglophone appetite for the parallel canon (see our ADISQ feature) have made L'Heptade increasingly discoverable. Discover it. It's one of ours. It always was.

Why it matters

Harmonium matters because they're the proof of the parallel universe's artistic depth: Quebec didn't just sustain stars and a scene — it sustained masterpieces, album-length art of the first rank, in French, on its own terms. L'Heptade belongs in any honest conversation about the greatest Canadian albums. It just sings in a language the conversation keeps forgetting it speaks.

Frequently asked questions

What is Harmonium’s masterpiece?

L’Heptade (1976) — the double-LP prog-folk suite widely regarded as the greatest album in Quebec history.

What is “Pour un instant”?

Harmonium’s signature early hit (1974) — a folk-pop standard and the band’s most-covered song.

Why is Harmonium unknown outside Quebec?

Language: a francophone prog-folk double album was a massive commercial phenomenon in Quebec but crossed the language border barely at all — a gap the streaming era is finally closing.
