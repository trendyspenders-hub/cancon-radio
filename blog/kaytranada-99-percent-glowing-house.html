Kaytranada: The Kid Who Made House Music Glow

Montreal has exported many sounds, but none warmer than this one. Kaytranada — Louis Kevin Celestin, a Haitian-Canadian producer who started making beats as a teenager in the city's suburbs — took house music's machinery and ran it through R&B's heart, creating a glow that won him a Grammy, a Polaris Prize, and a global following that treats his drops like holidays. Here's the whole arc, in the numbers and the chapters.

The data box

Kaytranada by the numbers - Born: August 6, 1992, in Montreal — Haitian-Canadian, started producing as a young teen under the name Kaytradamus before shortening it. - The platform years: Uploads to SoundCloud and YouTube through the early 2010s built a devoted international following before any album — remixes of R&B and hip-hop tracks re-imagined with his signature bounce. - The debut: 99.9% (2016) — featuring Anderson .Paak ("Glowed Up"), AlunaGeorge, Craig David — won the Polaris Music Prize and the Best Dance/Electronic Album Grammy. - The follow-up: Bubba (2019) — featuring Kali Uchis, Pharrell, Tinashe, Estelle — won a second Grammy and confirmed the sound as a movement. - The signature: A hybrid of house, hip-hop, and R&B — swung drums, glowing synth pads, basslines that walk like a strut — instantly recognizable within four bars. - The circle: His brother Lou Phelps raps; his Montreal orbit feeds the city's scene as both inspiration and infrastructure.

Chapter one: the bedroom-to-world pipeline

Kaytranada's rise is the streaming-era fairy tale done right: a teenager with software and taste, uploading beats to the internet, building an audience one remix at a time while the industry looked elsewhere. By the time labels came calling, he already had what they sell — a global fanbase and a recognizable sound. The lesson repeated: in the 2010s, the border between a Montreal bedroom and the world's dance floors was a upload button.

Chapter two: 99.9% and the Polaris statement

The debut album formalized what the remixes promised. 99.9% placed Kaytranada's production — those swung, glowing, effortlessly funky tracks — alongside a dream list of vocalists, from Anderson .Paak's jazz-rap to Craig David's UK garage pedigree. The album's twin coronations — the Polaris (Canada's most prestigious music prize, historically allergic to dance music) and the Grammy — announced something bigger than a producer's breakthrough: the full arrival of Black Canadian electronic music as art music, judged and crowned on the country's biggest stages.

Chapter three: Bubba and beyond

The follow-up deepened rather than repeated: Bubba leaned further into collaboration — Pharrell, Kali Uchis — while keeping the drums unmistakably his. A second Grammy confirmed the first wasn't a novelty. Meanwhile, the influence spread: the "Kaytranada bounce" became production vocabulary across R&B, hip-hop, and pop, and Montreal's scene — long a dance-music city — gained its most globally influential beatmaker since the disco era.

Why he matters

  1. He made dance music prestigious in Canada. Two Grammys and a Polaris for instrumental-forward dance records — a category the Canadian establishment long treated as lesser — reset the hierarchy.
  2. He's the diaspora sound, perfected. Haitian-Canadian, Montreal-raised, globally fluent: his music embodies the city's actual makeup rather than its postcard.
  3. The glow is a place. Ask fans to describe the Kaytranada sound and the same word keeps coming up — warm. In an era of cold maximalism, he made house music feel like sunlight through curtains. That's not production. That's authorship.

Frequently asked questions

What did Kaytranada win the Polaris Prize for?

99.9% (2016), his debut album — followed by Grammy wins for Best Dance/Electronic Album for both 99.9% and Bubba.

Where is Kaytranada from?

Montreal — born there in 1992 to Haitian-Canadian parents; he began producing as a teenager and built his first audience online.

What is Kaytranada’s sound?

A hybrid of house, hip-hop, and R&B: swung, bouncy drums, warm synth textures, and basslines built for both headphones and dance floors.
