Patrick Watson: Building Songs Like Cathedrals

Some musicians write songs; Patrick Watson constructs them — layer by layer, from whisper to wall of sound, in a process closer to architecture than pop craft. The Montreal-based singer-pianist (and his ever-shifting band of the same name) has spent two decades building some of the most ambitious music in the country: songs that start with a voice and a piano and end, four minutes later, somewhere between a folk ballad and a cathedral choir. It's a method, a signature, and a minor miracle every time it works — which is nearly always.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the method

Watson's process is the story: songs assembled in the studio like buildings — found sounds, layered harmonies, instruments arriving one at a time until the structure holds? — keep it clean: found sounds and layered harmonies assembled until the structure holds — then performed live with the same cumulative drama, the band adding instruments mid-song, the volume rising like a tide. The early records (Waterproof Weekend) built the cult; the voice — a fragile, keening instrument that can crest into something oceanic — became one of the most recognizable in Canadian music. Critics reached for the word cinematic; Watson earned it.

Chapter two: Close to Paradise (2006)

The Polaris-winning Close to Paradise (2006) was the breakthrough: "Luscious Life," "The Great Escape" (a song so absorbing it became the title of a documentary about the band? — keep it clean: a song that soundtracked the era's most atmospheric moments), "Sleeping Beauty" — songs that treated the album as a place, not a collection. The record beat bigger-selling competition for the Polaris (see our Polaris features), announcing that Canada's most prestigious music prize would reward ambition, not just commerce — a precedent that shaped the prize's whole identity. Watson had become the standard-bearer for building in an era of singles.

Chapter three: the ongoing construction

The catalog since has extended the architecture: Wooden Arms (2009), Adventures in Your Own Backyard (2012) — a deliberate retreat from grandeur toward intimacy? — keep it clean: a deliberate broadening of the palette — Love Songs for Robots (2015), Wave (2019), and onward: each record a new wing on the same building, each tour a demonstration that the studio's impossible constructions can, with enough musicians and nerve, be played by humans in rooms. Two decades in, Watson remains what he was at the start: a builder, unsentimental? — keep it clean: a builder — patient, exacting, and allergic to the easy version. The cathedrals keep going up. The congregation keeps growing.

Why he matters

Watson matters because he represents the craft-maximalist pole of Canadian indie — the proof that the country's scene could sustain an artist whose ambitions were sonic and architectural rather than commercial, and whose Polaris win helped define what the prize means. Some artists chase the moment. Watson builds for the ages, one layer at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What is Patrick Watson’s best album?

Close to Paradise (2006) — the Polaris Prize winner and consensus landmark.

Who is Patrick Watson?

A Montreal-based singer, pianist, and songwriter (fronting a band of the same name) known for painstakingly layered, cinematic art-pop.

What did Patrick Watson win the Polaris for?

Close to Paradise (2006) — a win that helped establish the prize’s reward-the-ambitious identity.
