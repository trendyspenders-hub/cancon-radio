The Toronto Sound: How the 6 Became the Center of the Music World

Every decade, the music industry crowns a "sound" that belongs to a city — Motown had Detroit, grunge had Seattle, bounce had New Orleans. The 2010s belonged to Toronto. Not as a quirky indie outpost, but as the commercial center of global pop and hip-hop. Here's how the 6 did it, and why its template — moody, melodic, self-made — still runs the charts.

BY CANCON RADIO5 MIN READBY THE NUMBERS

The sound, defined

Strip a Toronto hit down and you'll find shared DNA:

  • Atmospheric production — spacious, minor-key, nocturnal. Noah "40" Shebib's patented "underwater" mixing (heavy low end, drowned hi-hats) became the genre's watermark.
  • Melody over aggression — singing and rapping blur into one melodic flow.
  • Emotional transparency — vulnerability as a flex, long before it was mainstream.
  • DIY first — mixtapes on blogs, then free EPs, then the world. Labels arrived after the audience did.

DATA BOX: The Toronto Sound by the numbers

  • 2011 — Drake's Take Care and The Weeknd's House of Balloons land in the same year. The template is set.
  • 2020 — The Weeknd's "Blinding Lights" becomes the biggest Billboard Hot 100 song of the year and one of the most-streamed tracks in history.
  • Three solo Canadian artists have topped 100+ million records sold globally in the streaming era: Drake, The Weeknd, and Justin Bieber — all Toronto-adjacent.
  • 2015–present — Toronto producers (Boi-1da, WondaGurl, Murda Beatz, Sevn Thomas) shape hits for Beyoncé, Jay-Z, Rihanna, and Travis Scott.

The origin story nobody predicted

Toronto's rise doesn't start with a record executive. It starts with a TV show. Aubrey Graham spent his teens on Degrassi: The Next Generation — a genuinely Canadian institution — while quietly recording mixtapes. In February 2009 he released So Far Gone as a free download. "Best I Ever Had" crossed over from blog curiosity to radio smash in months, and the industry's compass tilted north.

The same year, Abel Tesfaye — The Weeknd — began anonymously uploading songs to YouTube. House of Balloons (2011) arrived like a rumor: Toronto's sonic identity (cold synths, longing, 3 a.m. regret) fully formed before anyone knew his face.

The lesson of 2011: Toronto didn't wait for permission. The city's artists treated the internet as the radio the CRTC couldn't regulate, and the world as their first market.

Why Toronto? Four ingredients

1. The diaspora advantage

Toronto is one of the most multicultural cities on earth, and its sound proves it. Caribbean rhythm (dancehall's influence on Drake's flow), Ethiopian and Eritrean melodic lines, South Asian and Middle Eastern production textures — the Toronto sound is a remix of the city's neighborhoods. Daniel Caesar's gospel-soul, Jessie Reyez's Colombian-Canadian R&B, and the city's dominant Afrobeats and dancehall playlists all draw from the same well.

2. A producer academy

Toronto built a self-contained hit-making pipeline:

  • Noah "40" Shebib — engineered the atmospheric blueprint
  • Boi-1da — Drake's primary beat architect since Comeback Season
  • WondaGurl — signed beats to Jay-Z at 16
  • Murda Beatz — Fort Erie's trap ambassador to the world
  • Nineteen85 / Sevn Thomas / Frank Dukes — the connective tissue between Toronto and pop's A-list

3. OVO as a city-state

Drake's OVO (October's Very Own) isn't just a label — it's a festival (OVO Fest), a clothing brand, a radio residency, and a flag. It gave Toronto artists a finish line inside the city instead of a plane ticket to New York or LA. The Weeknd's XO, PartyNextDoor, Roy Woods, dvsn, and Majid Jordan all benefited from a scene that could incubate at home.

4. The cold

Nobody jokes about the weather in Toronto music journalism for a reason. Long winters, big warehouses, cheap rent (once) — the city rewarded staying inside and making records. The "Toronto sound" is, in part, the sound of a city that goes indoors in November.

The branches of the Toronto tree

The sound stopped belonging to one genre years ago:

  • R&B's center of gravity moved north — The Weeknd's pop domination, Daniel Caesar's Freudian ("Get You"), Jessie Reyez's confessional work, Charlotte Cardin's French-English crossover.
  • Pop's biggest stars kept Toronto as home base — Justin Bieber (Stratford, via Toronto's studio circuit) and Shawn Mendes (Pickering) built global teen-pop careers without relocating.
  • The producer diaspora — a Toronto co-production credit is now so common on hit records that "produced by a kid from the GTA" is barely newsworthy.

What the rest of Canada should steal

Toronto's lesson isn't "be Toronto." It's that scenes need: (1) a distinct local sound worth identifying, (2) a generation that releases constantly, (3) infrastructure that keeps money and credit in the city, and (4) a flagship artist who waves the local flag globally. Vancouver, Montreal, Winnipeg, and Halifax have all had pieces of that formula at different times — Toronto just assembled all four at once.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Toronto called "the 6"?

The nickname comes from the city's two main area codes, 416 and 647 — "the 6" and "the 6ix." Drake popularized it globally around 2014–2015, and it stuck as both brand and identity.

Who started the Toronto sound?

No single artist, but Drake and The Weeknd crystallized it in 2011 (Take Care, House of Balloons), with producer Noah "40" Shebib as the sound's chief architect.

Is Daniel Caesar part of the Toronto sound?

Yes — he's the R&B branch of the same tree. His 2017 album Freudian came from Toronto's gospel and church-music community and earned Grammy recognition, extending the city's reach into soul.
