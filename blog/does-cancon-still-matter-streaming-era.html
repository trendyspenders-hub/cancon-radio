Does CanCon Radio Still Matter in the Streaming Era?

In 1971, the CRTC's CanCon rules reshaped a dial everyone listened to. In the 2020s, the dial is a legacy device: most music discovery happens through algorithms that owe nothing to the CRTC and owe everything to engagement data. So does the great Canadian cultural intervention still matter? The honest answer requires arguing both sides — because both are right about different things.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READTHE CASE FOR AND AGAINST

The case for: the rules built the machine everyone now uses

CanCon built the industry; streaming is just its newest room

Every structural advantage Canadian artists enjoy in the streaming era — the labels, the producers, the grant systems (FACTOR), the award infrastructure, the export pipelines — descends from the demand the 1971 regulations created. Remove the rules and you don't just change radio; you unwind fifty years of ecosystem. Drake, the Weeknd, Justin Bieber, and Tate McRae didn't need quotas to stream well, but the studios, engineers, and label staff around them exist because the ecosystem was regulated into existence.

Discovery is still gatekept — the gates just moved

The argument that “algorithms are neutral” dissolves on contact: playlists are editorial products, and global playlists default to global hits. Without rules pushing platforms to surface Canadian artists, the default is cultural gravity — 10% of North America's market absorbing 90% of the attention. The Online Streaming Act's core logic — make platforms contribute to and promote Canadian content — is CanCon's 1971 logic applied to 2020s infrastructure. The venue changed; the problem didn't.

Small markets need structural protection, full stop

Every country with a smaller cultural market than its neighbor protects it — France, Australia, South Korea. The question was never whether to intervene but how. Abandoning the field because “the internet is global” would be the first time in history a small-market culture won by disarming.

The case against: the rules fight the last war

The audience left before the rules arrived

CanCon governs broadcasters while listening migrated to platforms that, for a decade, weren't covered at all. You can mandate 35% of a radio dial nobody under thirty uses; you cannot easily mandate an algorithm's soul. The rules risk becoming a subsidy for yesterday's infrastructure — protecting radio's Canadian content obligations while the actual audience streams from Miami servers.

Streaming rewards who streaming rewards

The evidence cuts against the rules' premise: Canada's biggest streaming stars are global by nature, not by quota. Drake doesn't need discovery help. The artists who do need help — the mid-tier, the regional, the experimental — are poorly served by a framework built around radio spins and MAPL points, not playlist equity and sync placements. The 1971 toolkit fits 1971 problems.

Mandating “Canadian” on a platform is a definitional mess

What is Canadian content on TikTok? A Vancouver artist singing in English produced in LA? A Punjabi-Canadian artist in Brampton with global distribution? MAPL was already a famously awkward test; applying it to short-form video, producer-led beats, and borderless collaboration risks absurdity — and enforcement that picks winners by paperwork.

The verdict: mattered, matters, must change

Both cases land on the same conclusion from opposite directions. CanCon still matters — the ecosystem it built is why Canada punches above its weight on every global platform — but the 1971 mechanism matters less every year the audience stays gone from radio. The future of Canadian content policy looks less like a quota and more like what the Online Streaming Act gestures toward: platform obligations, discoverability requirements, and funding tied to where attention actually lives. The dial built the industry. The algorithm will decide whether the industry keeps the dial's lessons.

Frequently asked questions

Do Canadian content rules apply to streaming?

Not as quotas — but the Online Streaming Act (Bill C-11, 2023) requires major streaming platforms to contribute to and promote Canadian content, with CRTC implementation ongoing.

Is Canadian radio still important for breaking artists?

Less each year — it remains relevant for certain formats and demographics, but playlist placement and short-form video now drive most discovery.

What's the best argument for keeping CanCon rules?

The ecosystem argument: the rules built the labels, grants, and infrastructure that produce globally competitive artists, and abandoning them risks that entire inheritance.
