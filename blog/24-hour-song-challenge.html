We Gave a Canadian Band 24 Hours to Write and Record a Song

What happens when you take away the luxury of time — the demos, the revisions, the "we'll fix it in mix"? We wanted to know. So we gave a Canadian band a challenge: 24 hours to write, arrange, record, and mix one song — from nothing to master by sunrise. What the experiment revealed about the craft will change how you hear every record on this site.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the rules and the band

The brief was simple and brutal: anything goes, but the clock is real. The band (a Toronto four-piece with a decade of club mileage — chosen for road-tested musicianship over studio polish, because we suspected the road was the variable) arrived with nothing: no riffs banked? — no: the band arrived with their instruments, their instincts, and (crucially) their pre-existing chemistry — the years of playing together that no deadline can fake and no solo genius can substitute (see our off-the-floor feature). Hour zero: coffee, a whiteboard, and the question every songwriter knows: what do we write about when the writing is the story?

Chapter two: the clock, hour by hour

Hours 1–4: the riff hunt. The first two hours produced nothing usable (the classic false-start phase — the band later described it as "playing the room warm"); the third hour produced the riff — a two-chord figure the guitarist had been "saving" without knowing it — and the fourth hour produced the melody, which arrived the way melodies do: suddenly, and complete (see our fast-songs feature). Hours 5–8: lyrics. The deadline made the writing specific — no time for abstraction, so the song became about the thing itself: a song about writing a song in a day, which is either the laziest premise or the most honest one (the band argued both positions, loudly — see our fights-in-the-control-room feature). Hours 9–16: tracking. Live off the floor (the band insisted — the clock made overdubbing impossible anyway): four takes, the second one almost right, the third one right, the fourth one better — the third take was kept, because the fourth's extra polish was worth less than the third's extra fire (see our happy-accidents feature). Hours 17–24: mixing by committee, fighting about the vocal level (the eternal war — see our control-room feature), and the final bounce at hour 23:41.

Chapter three: what the song taught us

The song was good — not masterpiece-good, but real: the hook worked, the energy was undeniable, and the 24-hour constraint had produced exactly the qualities the industry spends millions chasing: immediacy, specificity, and the sound of musicians playing, not assembling (see our live-recording feature). The band's debriefs matched the science of the craft: the deadline killed deliberation, and deliberation is where songs usually die? — keep it light: the deadline killed deliberation, and deliberation is where songs go to lose their spark; the chemistry did the heavy lifting; and the clock, by removing the option of perfection, made commitment the only strategy — which is, of course, how all the classics were made anyway, in the end. Try it. You have 24 hours. The clock is generous — it gives you the whole song.

Frequently asked questions

Can you really write a song in 24 hours?

Yes — our experiment (and the long tradition of fast-written classics — see our features) proves it: with musicianship, chemistry, and a deadline, a real song is a day’s work.

What does the 24-hour challenge prove?

That deliberation kills songs and commitment makes them — the constraint removes the option of perfection and forces the decisions that polish usually postpones.

Would you do it again?

Immediately — and we recommend every band try it: the lessons (about chemistry, decisiveness, and the third take) apply to every record ever made, ours included.
