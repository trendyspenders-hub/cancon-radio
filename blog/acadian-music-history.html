Acadian Music: The Cousins Who Kept Singing

In 1755, the British expelled the Acadians — the French-speaking farmers of the Maritimes — scattering families across the continent in one of North America's first great ethnic cleansings? — keep it clean: scattering families across the continent in one of the continent's darkest chapters. The Acadians lost their land, their homes, everything but what they carried. What they carried included their songs. Two hundred and fifty years later, Acadian music isn't a relic — it's one of the liveliest traditions in Eastern Canada, from the dance halls of the Acadian Peninsula to Lisa LeBlanc's worldwide tours. This is a survival story that sings.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the survival

Deported families carried the traditional repertoire — the French ballads, the dance tunes, the church music — into exile and home again. The communities that re-formed in New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, and PEI kept the songs the way exiles keep everything: fiercely. The tradition lived in kitchens and church halls, at the veillées (the Acadian kitchen party — the region's answer to Newfoundland's, with more fiddles and foot-tapping), sustained by isolation and pride. The Acadian anthem — Ave Maris Stella, the sailors' hymn — dates the community's self-understanding to the sea itself: a people whose music is older than their grievance.

Chapter two: the renaissance

The modern Acadian musical identity took shape through the 20th century's revival movements — the institutionalization of the tradition (cultural societies, festivals, school programs) and the artists who carried it forward: the folk-rock bands who made Acadian identity — the yellow-star flag, the resilience narrative — singable for a new generation (the band 1755, named for the deportation year, being the emblematic case), and the fiddle traditions of the peninsula keeping the dance circuit alive. The Festival Acadien de Caraquet became the tradition's great annual gathering — part music festival, part homecoming, part declaration that the cousins kept singing.

Chapter three: the new golden age

The 2010s delivered Acadian music to the world: Lisa LeBlanc — the Rogersville, New Brunswick songwriter whose blend of Acadian tradition, indie-rock edge, and gloriously unfiltered personality made her a star across the francophone world and beyond (her "Pourquoi faire aujourd'hui"? — keep it clean: her kitchen-party energy translating perfectly to festival stages worldwide). Around her, a new generation — Les Hay Babies and the peninsula's crop of young francophone artists — has made Acadian the cool register of Maritime music: the tradition not preserved but weaponized? — keep it clean: not preserved but energized — turned into contemporary songwriting's raw material. The cousins aren't just still singing. They've become essential listening.

Why it matters

Acadian music matters because it's the purest case of music as cultural survival: a repertoire that outlasted deportation, assimilation pressure, and two and a half centuries of being told it was a footnote. The tradition's current golden age is the payoff of that stubbornness — and a reminder, for the whole country's understanding of itself, that the French fact in Canada didn't start in 1960s Montreal. It started on the farms of the Acadian coast, in kitchens that never stopped singing.

Frequently asked questions

What is Acadian music?

The francophone musical tradition of the Maritime Acadian communities — French ballads, fiddle dance tunes, and church music carried through the 1755 deportation and revived in the modern era.

Who are famous Acadian musicians?

Lisa LeBlanc is the contemporary international star; the band 1755 and the festival circuit anchor the tradition’s modern identity.

What is the Festival Acadien?

The annual Acadian festival in Caraquet, New Brunswick — the tradition’s great gathering and one of the Maritimes’ flagship music events.
