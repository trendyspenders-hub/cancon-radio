The CanCon Regulations of 1971: The Rule That Built Canadian Music

Every country with a broadcast system has a version of the same panic: our airwaves belong to us, but everything on them comes from somewhere else. Canada's answer, unveiled on January 18, 1971, was the most aggressive cultural policy in the Western world — a quota system that forced radio stations to play Canadian music whether they wanted to or not. Half a century later, the argument it started is still the central argument in Canadian music.

BY CANCON RADIO4 MIN READA TIMELINE

The timeline

1968The diagnosis

The Fowler Commission on broadcasting confirms what anyone with a radio already knows: Canadian stations program almost exclusively British and American records, and Canadian artists who can't get played at home don't get played anywhere. The machinery of the solution — the Canadian Radio-television Commission, later the CRTC — is born.

January 18, 1971 — The quota

The CRTC announces its Canadian content regulations: AM radio stations must play a minimum of 30% Canadian music, with FM (then mostly easy-listening and classical) held to 25%. The industry howls. Programmers insist Canadian records aren't good enough, that audiences will tune out, that the free market is being mugged. Within a decade, the howling is replaced by gold records.

The MAPL system — how a song becomes Canadian

The regulations need a test, and the test becomes famous: MAPL. A recording can qualify as Canadian if it meets at least two of four criteria — M: music composed by a Canadian; A: the artist performing is Canadian; P: the performance is recorded in Canada; L: lyrics written by a Canadian. The MAPL logo becomes a permanent piece of Canadian graphic design. (The debate about what MAPL gets wrong — an American band recording a Canadian songwriter's song in Los Angeles can qualify; a Toronto band cutting a cover in Toronto might not — begins immediately and never ends.)

1971–1980The proof of concept

The regulations do exactly what they were designed to do: create demand where none existed. Canadian labels sign acts they'd previously ignored; Canadian radio gives steady rotation to Anne Murray, Gordon Lightfoot, the Guess Who, and Bachman-Turner Overdrive. The first wave of CanCon hits proves audiences don't flee Canadian records; they were simply never offered them before.

The 1980s — FM follows, and the quota rises

As FM becomes the dominant music band, the 30% quota extends across the dial. The new wave explosion of the 80s — Corey Hart, Men Without Hats, Platinum Blonde, Glass Tiger, Loverboy — is a CanCon dividend: domestic acts get the repetition cycle that builds stars. MuchMusic launches in 1984, adding the missing visual layer, and by the late 80s Canadian content on Canadian radio sits at 30% as a floor — and far higher on some stations and dayparts.

199935%

The CRTC raises the minimum to 35%, where it effectively sits today, with genre-specific exemptions for formats like classical and jazz. The industry, by now, barely objects. CanCon has become an ecosystem: FACTOR funding, video grants, the Junos, MuchMusic — a whole parallel economy built atop the original quota.

The streaming era — the rules bend, then break

For decades the loophole debate ran on niceties. Then the audience left radio. Streaming services — Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube — weren't "broadcasters" under the old law, which meant the 35% quota simply didn't apply to where most listening had gone. Canadian artists now compete for playlist slots on the same terms as the entire planet, which for Drake and the Weeknd is a triumph and for everyone else is a crisis. Parliament's answer, the Online Streaming Act (Bill C-11, 2023), extends CRTC authority to streaming platforms, requiring them to contribute to and promote Canadian content. Whether "Canadian content" means anything on an algorithm is the question the next fifty years will answer.

What CanCon actually built

Detractors said quotas would produce mediocrity; the record says the opposite. The regulations created demand, and demand created infrastructure: Canadian A&R departments, Canadian producers (Bob Ezrin, David Foster, Bruce Fairbairn), Canadian studios, a Canadian star system, and an export pipeline. Lightfoot, Young, Mitchell, Cohen, the Hip, Alanis, Shania, Drake — none of them needed a quota to be great, but the system behind them, from first radio spin to Juno to world stage, was built by the rule of 1971. The most honest summary is also the simplest: before CanCon, Canadian radio barely played Canadians; after CanCon, Canadian music became one of the country's most successful cultural exports. Whatever its flaws, the quota built the industry everyone now argues about.

The arguments that never end

  1. "Quotas protect mediocrity." Fifty years of CanCon hits say the quota mostly exposed excellence the market was ignoring. The duds existed — but they existed on American radio too.
  2. "MAPL is a silly test." It is! It's also the only test that survived contact with lawyers, and its famous absurdities (a foreign band covering a Canadian song in Toronto = Canadian) are survivable.
  3. "What does Canadian content mean on Spotify?" Nobody knows yet. The streaming rules will be litigated, lobbied, and rewritten for a decade. The 1971 fight never ended; it changed venues.

Frequently asked questions

What are the CanCon regulations?

Canadian content requirements introduced by the CRTC on January 18, 1971, mandating that radio stations play a minimum percentage of Canadian music — starting at 25–30% and rising to 35% by 1999.

What does MAPL stand for?

Music, Artist, Performance, Lyrics — the four criteria used to determine whether a recording qualifies as Canadian. A song needs at least two of the four.

Do CanCon rules apply to Spotify?

Not historically — but the Online Streaming Act (2023) extended CRTC authority to streaming services, requiring Canadian content promotion and contributions. How that works in practice is still being defined.
