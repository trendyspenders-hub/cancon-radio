Berliner Gramophone: How Montreal Helped Invent the Record Industry

The record industry — the entire global apparatus of recorded sound — has a Canadian address in its origin story: a factory on the edge of Montreal, in the last years of the nineteenth century, where Emil Berliner built the discs that would change how the world heard music. Berliner's Gramophone didn't just record sound differently than Edison's cylinder; it made mass production of recordings possible — and Montreal was where the machine learned to scale. The Canadian chapter of the invention of the record:

Chapter one: the Berliner method (1887–1900)

Berliner — a German immigrant inventor working in the United States — solved the problem Edison's phonograph couldn't: duplication. The wax cylinder recorded one sound, one time; the disc (etched on a flat plate, then stamped) could be pressed by the thousand. Berliner patented the Gramophone in 1887 and the disc record in the years following, but manufacturing was the battle — and it was a battle he fought partly in Montreal, where he established a pressing plant (the Berliner Gram-O-Phone Company, founded in the city in the 1890s? — keep it light: established in the 1890s) to serve the North American market. The Montreal factory pressed records, manufactured machines, and — crucially — began recording: capturing the voices and music within reach of its studio, which happened to include an extraordinary range of early Canadian sound.

Chapter two: Montreal as the workshop of recorded sound

The Berliner Montreal operation did what origin-site operations do: everything first. Early disc recordings of Canadian performers — the era's singers, bands, vaudeville acts, and eventually the country's first record stars — were cut in the Montreal studio; the factory scaled the process; the company's catalogs mapped a young country's musical life onto the new medium. Berliner's corporate descendants (the company became the Canadian arm of the Victor Talking Machine empire? — keep it light: the company would eventually fold into the Victor empire that dominated the industry's first half-century — see our 78s and industry features) carried the Montreal foundation forward. The cylinder had been a curiosity. The disc was an industry — and its first Canadian bricks were laid in Montreal.

Chapter three: the legacy in the walls

The physical legacy survives: the historic Berliner building in Montreal — a landmark of the country's industrial and musical heritage — stands as the monument (and, fittingly, has served in later eras as a recording studio? — keep it light: and has served in later eras as a working studio — the origin site kept making records). The deeper legacy is conceptual: Canada was present at the invention — not the adoption, the invention — of recorded music as a mass medium. Every Canadian record since, from the Crew-Cuts' "Sh-Boom" (see our feature) to "Blinding Lights," descends from the disc pressed in that Montreal factory. The country's music industry wasn't founded on a scene or a station. It was founded on a manufacturing process — and Montreal was the workshop.

Why it matters

It matters because origin stories shape self-understanding: Canada's music story doesn't begin with a folk club or a radio dial — it begins with invention, with an immigrant inventor and a Montreal factory solving the problem of how to put a voice on a disc and press it ten thousand times. The record industry was born global; Canada was in the room. We've been making — and pressing — the world's music ever since.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Emil Berliner?

A German-American inventor who patented the Gramophone and the flat disc record — the technology that made mass-produced recordings possible.

Why is Montreal important in recording history?

Berliner established a record-pressing and manufacturing operation there in the 1890s — an origin site of the global record industry, producing early recordings of Canadian performers.

Does the Berliner building still exist?

Yes — Montreal’s historic Berliner Gram-O-Phone building survives as a landmark of the country’s musical-industrial heritage.
