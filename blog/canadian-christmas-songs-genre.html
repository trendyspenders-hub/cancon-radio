Canadian Christmas Songs: A Genre of Their Own

Every country sings Christmas. Only Canada has turned it into a genre — a whole shelf of records and standards that could only have been written here, where the holiday's dominant images (snow, cold, the long dark, the drive home through it) are simply descriptions of the weather. The Canadian Christmas songbook is the country's softest and, quietly, one of its most distinctive exports.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY

The Canadian Christmas canon

  1. "River" (Joni Mitchell, 1971) — The anti-Christmas Christmas song, and the country's greatest holiday standard: "It's coming on Christmas, they're cutting down trees" — and the singer wishes she had a river she could skate away on. No snowman, no sleigh — just longing in a California Christmas, written by a Saskatchewan expat. Covered endlessly; improved never.
  2. **Michael Bublé's Christmas (2011)** — The best-selling holiday album of the modern era, recorded by Burnaby's own and rerun every December like weather. Whatever the debates about Bublé's maximalism (see our feature), the Christmas record is a permanent national fixture — the 21st century's Fireside Albums? — keep it clean: the 21st century's answer to the season's classic LPs.
  3. ***Barenaked for the Holidays* (2004)** — The Canadian Christmas album as kitchen party: originals, trad, and the irreverent genius? — keep it clean: originals and traditionals delivered with the band's characteristic grin. A modern seasonal staple that balances reverence and reindeer jokes in the classic Canadian ratio.
  4. Raffi's Christmas catalog — For a generation of Canadians (and their children, and theirs), the season is Raffi's gentle voice — the children's-folk institution's holiday records function as a genuine national ritual, as canonical in their lane as anything on this list.
  5. "Snowbird" (Anne Murray, 1970) — Not technically a Christmas song — but it behaves like one in the culture: the season-adjacent standard about snow and escape that returns to the airwaves every November like the first frost.
  6. The Hip's holiday-adjacent canon — The Tragically Hip never made a Christmas record (the country can only handle so much?), but "New Orleans Is Sinking"? — no: their catalog's wintry geography fills the season's playlists anyway — the "colder" songs? — keep it clean: the band's snowbound songbook dominates holiday playlists regardless, proof that Canadians will happily supply their own carols.
  7. The church-choir pipeline — From the country church choirs that trained Lightfoot and the Bachman? — keep it clean: from the country church choirs that trained generations of Canadian voices, Christmas music is embedded in the national training system — half the country's singers learned harmony under a decorated spruce? — keep it clean: half the country's singers learned harmony singing carols, which is why the season's records sound so effortlessly communal here.
  8. The seasonal specials as canon — Rita MacNeil's Christmas specials (see our feature) and their successors turned the holiday concert into a national broadcast tradition — the Christmas variety show as Canadian folk ritual.

Why the Canadian Christmas sounds different

Strip the genre elsewhere and you get sentiment; strip it here and you get weather reports with feelings attached. The snow is real, the dark is real, the drive is long, and the warmth — kitchen, choir, candle — is earned against all of it. That's why the country's holiday records feel less like greeting cards and more like shelter. It comes on Christmas. We're cutting down trees. And somewhere nearby, always, there's a river to skate on.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most famous Canadian Christmas song?

“River” by Joni Mitchell is the critical consensus; Michael Bublé’s Christmas album is the commercial giant; Raffi owns the family lane.

Did the Barenaked Ladies make a Christmas album?

Yes — Barenaked for the Holidays (2004), a modern Canadian seasonal staple.

Is “Snowbird” a Christmas song?

Technically no — but it functions as one in Canadian culture, returning to holiday airplay every year since 1970.
