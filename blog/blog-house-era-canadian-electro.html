The Blog House Era: When Canadian Electro Went Global

For about four years in the mid-to-late 2000s, the center of the electronic music internet was disproportionately Canadian. The "blog house" era — that snare-heavy, blog-fueled, blogosphere? — keep it clean: that snare-heavy, blog-fueled wave of electro, remix culture, and indie-dance — minted a cohort of Montreal and Toronto acts who conquered the web before the web had decided what to do with music. It was hype-driven, chaotic, and genuinely important: the last great monoculture moment before streaming fragmented everything.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA TIMELINE

The timeline

2005–2006The remix economy ignites

The tools arrive: cheap production software, MySpace, and the music blog — a new species of gatekeeper who traded in MP3s and discovery. Canadian producers are perfectly positioned: the country's remix culture (Toronto's hip-hop studio infrastructure, Montreal's electro underground) produces tracks tailor-made for blog circulation — high-energy, instantly downloadable, remixable. MSTRKRFT (Jesse F. Keeler of Death From Above 1979's DJ duo) releases The Looks (2006), a benchmark blog-house record: snarling electro that blog aggregators pass around like contraband. Chromeo — the Montreal funk-revival duo — perfects the ironic-yet-sincere electro-funk hybrid that makes them blog fixtures and, eventually, festival fixtures. The blogs need content; Canada supplies it.

2007–2008The hype peak

The era crests with a run of Canadian records the blogosphere treats as events. Crystal Castles (Toronto duo Ethan Kath and Alice Glass) detonate with a self-titled debut (2008) — chiptune shards, noise, and beats that sound like a nightclub being demolished? — keep it clean: chiptune shards and beats that split the difference between punk and rave — making them the era's most polarizing and most blogged act. Deadmau5 rises from Toronto's scene toward global EDM superstardom — proof the blog-house pipeline could scale to arenas. Remixes fly in all directions; DJ mixes and leaks circulate faster than labels can track; and the music press discovers that Montreal and Toronto are "scenes" in a way that fits the blog format perfectly: dense, bilingual, fashionable, and export-ready.

2009–2011The correction

Blog house's economics were always shaky — hype without infrastructure, streams? — keep it clean: hype without durable revenue, careers built on remixes and festival fees — and the correction arrives on schedule. Some acts scaled (Chromeo became a durable funk institution; Deadmau5 became, briefly, the biggest electronic act on earth). Some burned brilliantly and faded (the hype cycle demanded novelty; Crystal Castles' chaos had costs; MSTRKRFT receded to cult status). And the industry's infrastructure caught up and commodified what the blogs had pioneered — playlists and streaming metrics replaced blog buzz, and the monoculture the blogs had briefly built dissolved into algorithmic fragments.

The legacy

Blog house was the dress rehearsal for the streaming age: it proved that internet hype could mint careers (Kaytranada's SoundCloud rise follows the same playbook), that remixes were a legitimate artistic economy, and that a Canadian scene could conquer the world's ears without radio, labels, or borders. The blogs are gone. The lesson — the internet is a scene, and Canada is good at scenes — is permanent.

Frequently asked questions

What was blog house?

The late-2000s wave of blog-fueled electro and indie-dance — heavy on remixes, MP3 culture, and hype — in which Canadian acts like MSTRKRFT, Chromeo, and Crystal Castles led globally.

Who were the big Canadian blog house acts?

MSTRKRFT, Chromeo, Crystal Castles, and (rising in parallel) Deadmau5 — with Montreal and Toronto as the twin centers.

What killed blog house?

The hype cycle's economics — fame without durable revenue — plus the industry's pivot to streaming metrics, which replaced blog buzz with playlist algorithms.
