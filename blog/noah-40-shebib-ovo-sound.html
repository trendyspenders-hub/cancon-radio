Noah "40" Shebib: The Quiet Sound of OVO

Every musical empire has an architect whose name the casual fan can't quite place. For Toronto's global takeover, that architect is Noah "40" Shebib — Drake's collaborator since before the fame, the co-founder of OVO Sound, and the producer who gave the 6 its sonic signature: nocturnal, spacious, 808-heavy, and sung from inside a feeling. If Drake is the voice of the era, 40 built the room the voice echoes in.

The data box: 40 in numbers

Noah "40" Shebib by the numbers - Born: March 31, 1983, Toronto — a child actor (his mother a teacher? — keep it clean: raised in Toronto with an early career on camera) who chose the studio over the set. - The partnership: Met a young Aubrey Graham in Toronto's mid-2000s scene; produced on So Far Gone (2009), Drake's breakout mixtape, and has been the primary sonic partner on nearly everything since. - The signature sound: "The 40 sound" — sub-heavy 808s with the highs shaved off? — keep it clean: sub-heavy low end, atmospheric pads, reverb-washed vocals pushed forward, negative space as an instrument. - The classics: "Marvins Room," "Started From the Bottom," "Hold On, We're Going Home," "Worst Behavior" — and the Take Care album as a whole, the statement of the sound. - The label: Co-founded OVO Sound, whose roster (PARTYNEXTDOOR, Majid Jordan, dvsn, Roy Woods) extends the 40 aesthetic into an entire scene. - The craft: An obsessive mixer — famously spending days on snare sounds — and a credited architect of modern rap's studio language.

Chapter one: from camera to console

Shebib's first career happened by accident: a Toronto kid booked as an actor. But the pull was always music — production software, basement studios, the technical obsession of someone who treats a mix like a physics problem. By his early 20s he was embedded in Toronto's underground, engineering for local rappers, building a reputation for taste in texture — the exact skill the coming decade would reward beyond measure.

Chapter two: the Drake partnership

When Shebib and Drake connected in the mid-2000s, they were peers in the same small scene — and the partnership that reshaped pop began the way the best ones do: as friendship plus mutual standard-raising. So Far Gone (2009) — with its hazy, sinking sound — announced both of them. Then came the imperial run: Thank Me Later, and especially Take Care (2011), the album where the "40 sound" became a genre: melancholy as luxury, minimalism as power, a kick drum like a heartbeat in an empty condo. The aesthetic was instantly influential — every major rap producer of the 2010s has a record with its fingerprints — and it traveled: the same spacious sorrow powered the Weeknd's adjacent rise and a thousand imitators worldwide.

Chapter three: OVO as a sound, not just a label

When OVO Sound formalized as a label, it did so with a clear aesthetic brief: 40's proteges and fellow travelers. PARTYNEXTDOOR's druggy slow jams, Majid Jordan's synth-R&B, dvsn's gospel-tinted bedroom confessionals — all extend the Toronto sound the way Motown's roster extended its house style. The genius is that it scales: one producer's sensibility, codified into a place — the way Hitsville or 54 Sound? — keep it clean: the way classic house styles turned geography into genre.

Chapter four: the quiet giant

Shebib's public profile stays deliberately low — no mythology-building, minimal interviews, the work as the statement. In an industry that rewards branding, he branded restraint: the producer as the room, not the voice. The result is one of the great behind-the-board careers in Canadian music history — the man who made Toronto sound like midnight, and then made midnight the most commercially successful mood on earth.

Frequently asked questions

What does Noah “40” Shebib produce?

Drake's catalog from So Far Gone onward is the core — "Marvins Room," "Started From the Bottom," "Hold On, We're Going Home," and the Take Care album are the signature records — plus OVO Sound's roster.

What is the “Toronto sound”?

The nocturnal, spacious aesthetic associated with 40's production: heavy low end, atmospheric textures, vocals drenched in reverb, and silence used as an instrument.

Is 40 still working with Drake?

Yes — the partnership remains the foundation of Drake's recorded output, with 40 credited across his albums into the present era.
