Second Acts: What Canadian Musicians Did Next

The music business chews through careers; Canada's musicians, characteristically, refuse to be chewed. Some of the country's most interesting cultural figures are musicians who walked off the stage and built something entirely different — or walked off and came back with a book, a winery, or a second TV career. The second act is a Canadian specialty. Here's the honor roll.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY

The second-act canon

  1. Hugh Dillon — The Headstones' sneer, perfected (see our feature), became one of Canada's most reliable acting faces: decades of film and television work running parallel to the band's reunion, and neither career treated as the side project. The sneer turned out to be a transferable skill.
  2. Maestro Fresh Wes — Canadian hip-hop's first gold record (see our feature) became a working actor with a decades-long screen career — one of the very few people in the country with two full careers in two different industries.
  3. K'naan — The "Wavin' Flag" phenomenon pivoted to writing and humanitarian work, including children's literature — a poet choosing quieter rooms after filling stadiums.
  4. Geddy Lee — Rush's frontman closed the band's story with a bestselling memoir (My Big Beautiful Book of Bass era — the memoir being 2023's My Effin' Life) — decades of craft, finally on the page. Few rock memoirs have been received with more affection.
  5. Robbie Robertson — The Band's architect spent his post-Last-Waltz decades as a film composer (Scorsese's go-to) and a memoirist (Testimony) — turning the mythology he'd built into literature.
  6. Seán McCann — Great Big Sea's founding voice left the arena circuit for solo work, advocacy, and recovery storytelling — the second act as mission (see our Newfoundland feature).
  7. David Foster — The producer's third act is brand-as-platform: television, discovery (Bublé, Groban), and the "David Foster" genre made institution.
  8. The producers' diaspora — Behind the scenes, the second act is the industry itself: Bob Rock, Daniel Lanois, and dozens of musician-producers whose "next" was shaping everyone else's records (see our producer features).
  9. The winemakers, restaurateurs, and innkeepers — From Niagara vineyards to PEI farm tables? — keep it clean: a veritable guild of Canadian musicians now pours the drinks they once sang about. The country runs on converted tour vans? — keep it clean: the country runs on musicians' second businesses more than it knows.
  10. The teachers — The quietest and maybe most important second act: the musicians who moved into education (Martha Johnson's music-classroom work; school-of-music founders from McLachlan's Vancouver institution onward), passing the whole tradition to the next generation.

Why the second act is so Canadian

Part of it is arithmetic: the domestic market rarely sustains a fifty-year performing career, so reinvention is survival. But the pattern runs deeper — a culture that treats careers as crafts rather than fame-vehicles produces people who keep making things when the spotlight moves. The musicians on this list didn't "leave music." They took the discipline, the taste, and the work ethic somewhere new. The craft traveled. It always does.

Frequently asked questions

Which Canadian musicians became actors?

Hugh Dillon (the Headstones) and Maestro Fresh Wes are the signature examples — both sustaining major screen careers alongside or after their music.

What musicians wrote bestselling memoirs?

Geddy Lee (My Effin' Life) and Robbie Robertson (Testimony) are the recent landmark rock memoirs; Dan Hill and k.d. lang have also published well-received books.

What do musicians do after fame?

The Canadian evidence: acting, writing, producing, teaching, entrepreneurship, and advocacy — often several at once, and often as durable as the music career itself.
