The Backstage Economy: The Jobs That Make Canadian Music Happen

The show is the visible tip; underneath it runs an invisible economy of jobs — the merch sellers, the stage managers, the guitar techs, the settlement runners — that every Canadian performance depends on and almost no audience can name. The backstage economy, catalogued: who they are, what they do, and why the whole thing collapses without them:

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY

The jobs, catalogued

  1. The stage manager — the show's air-traffic controller: the set times enforced, the changeovers choreographed, the "you're on in five" delivered with the calm of someone who has never once panicked. The show you watched ran on the stage manager's clipboard.
  2. The guitar/drum/backline tech — the instrument's keeper: the restring before the set, the amp that must work (the spare that will work — the drum kit tuned to the headliner's exact requirements). The guitar tech's hands are the reason the solo sounded effortless.
  3. The merch seller — the night's economist: the table set, the sizes organized, the card reader charged, the exact change for the cash customers: the merch table is where the band's profit lives (often the night's only reliable profit), and the seller is its bank, inventory manager, and brand ambassador.
  4. The monitor engineer — the musician's lifeline: the monitor mix (what the band hears) is a separate job from the house mix (what you hear) — and the monitor engineer's work is invisible precisely because it's perfect.
  5. The lighting designer/op — the night's painter: the rig programmed, the cues cued, the mood manufactured: the show's look is a designed object, and the LD is its author.
  6. The door staff and security — the night's first and last impression: the ID checks, the bag searches, the gentle removals: the venue's safety and the crowd's welcome, held by the people everyone forgets by set's end.
  7. The tour/production manager — the tour's brain: routing, settlement, crisis — the person who makes six thousand kilometers behave.
  8. The settlement runner / venue accountant — the night's paper: the door counted, the guarantee met or the percentage calculated, the night's math done at 1 a.m. in the office.
  9. The photographer/videographer — the night's memory: the shoot that becomes the band's next six months of content.
  10. The driver — the miles themselves: the bus captain, the gear-truck driver, the person who delivers the whole fragile enterprise across the distances — the job where the stakes are total and the visibility is zero.

The economy's truth

The backstage workforce is the industry's largest employment base — more people work the invisible jobs than the visible ones — and the least protected (the gig-to-gig contracts, the modest day rates, the no-credit culture). The next time the show runs perfectly, remember: it wasn't magic. The magic was a hundred people doing their jobs. The applause belongs to the tip. The economy holds up the tip. Thank the invisible ones. They're packing the truck.

Frequently asked questions

What jobs exist behind a concert?

Stage managers, techs (guitar/drum/backline), merch sellers, monitor engineers, lighting designers, door staff, tour/production managers, settlement staff, photographers, and drivers — the invisible workforce every show requires.

Who makes the money at a concert?

The artists (at scale); at the club level, often the merch table and the guarantee — the backstage workforce earns modest gig-to-gig day rates.

Why is the backstage economy important?

It's the live industry's largest employment base and its least visible — every show the audience enjoys is labor by people nobody applauds.
