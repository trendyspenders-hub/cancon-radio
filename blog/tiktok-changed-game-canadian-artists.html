How TikTok Changed the Game for Canadian Artists

No single force has reshaped Canadian music's economics this decade — not streaming, not radio reform — like a short-video app built on lip-syncs and fifteen-second hooks. TikTok didn't just find Canadian artists new audiences; it rewrote who gets discovered, what gets signed, and what a “hit” even means. Here's the timeline of the takeover.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA TIMELINE

The timeline

2018–2019The app arrives, and the first Canadians catch the wave

TikTok's international expansion coincides with the earliest Canadian breakout moments — unsigned or lightly signed artists watching a snippet of their song migrate into millions of videos overnight. The lesson lands fast: the hook is the new single. Choruses get written for the clip; 15 seconds of a song can matter more than the three minutes around it.

2020–2021The pandemic accelerator

Locked-down Canada goes online, and the algorithm becomes the country's biggest A&R department. Canadian examples pile up: Mother Mother's “Hayloft,” a 2008 deep cut resurrected into a global streaming phenomenon; Tate McRae's “you broke me first,” a bedroom-written ballad that became a streaming giant; Alessia Cara's career trajectory reshaped by viral momentum; dances, covers, and memes turning catalog songs into new chart records. Labels restructure entire departments around “TikTok teams”; signing decisions start with viral velocity.

2021–2022The industry reorganizes around the app

The feedback loop tightens: artists write for virality (some openly, some resentfully), radio adds songs the algorithm already broke, and the Junos and Polaris juries find themselves canonizing songs the culture discovered months earlier on the app. Canadian artists from every province and community — including Indigenous and diaspora scenes the old gatekeepers underserved — build global audiences without radio, labels, or industry permission. For the first time, the discovery machine doesn't care where you're from. That's genuinely new.

2023The reckoning

The backlash arrives on schedule: over-saturation of soundalike virality, artists publicly exhausted by algorithm-chasing, and the volatile geopolitics of the platform itself (including Canadian regulatory scrutiny and the ban debate). Meanwhile the hits keep coming — “greedy” (2023) moves from TikTok momentum to genuine global pop stardom for Tate McRae, completing the pipeline from app to arena.

2024–presentThe new normal

TikTok settles into the ecosystem as permanent infrastructure: no longer a novelty but the first filter through which most young listeners meet music. Canadian success stories now routinely begin with a clip — a kitchen performance, a 15-second chorus, a strange old song resurfacing. The old pipeline (campus radio → MuchMusic → label deal) is dead; the new one (clip → algorithm → playlist → deal) is borderless, and Canada — with its enormous per-capita creator base and diaspora advantage — keeps over-performing on it.

What TikTok actually changed

  1. The hook conquered the song. Fifteen seconds is the new chorus; arrangement economy is the new songwriting skill.
  2. Catalog became currency. Old songs (“Hayloft,” decades-old tracks) get second lives; the back catalog is now a launchpad, not a graveyard.
  3. The gatekeepers moved. A&R now listens to the algorithm; radio plays what the app broke; award shows canonize what the app discovered. Power followed attention.

Frequently asked questions

Which Canadian artists broke through TikTok?

Tate McRae, Mother Mother (via the “Hayloft” revival), Alessia Cara (momentum phase), and countless emerging artists across genres and communities — the app's Canadian breakout list grows monthly.

Is TikTok good or bad for Canadian music?

Both: it democratized discovery beyond radio and labels, but it also rewards virality over craft and makes careers hostage to an algorithm nobody controls.

Do labels still matter in the TikTok era?

Yes, but differently — they're now accelerants and infrastructure for momentum the app creates, rather than the originators of discovery. The deal happens after the audience, more often than before.
