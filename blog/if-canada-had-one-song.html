If Canada Had One Song, What Would It Be?

It's the question this entire website keeps circling, so let's have the argument properly. Not "best" — one. If the country had to be represented by a single recording, playable to any audience on earth, which song carries the whole map? Four candidates dominate every debate. Each fails differently. That's what makes this fun.

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READTHE CASE FOR AND AGAINST

The case for "Northwest Passage" (Stan Rogers)

For: It's the complete country in one composition — geography (the Arctic, the plains, the Rockies), history (Franklin, Amundsen), the journey motif (the road trip as national sacrament), and the thesis line about "tracing one warm line" — the road, the song, the life — through a land too big to hold. It's singable by a stadium or a kitchen. It's ours to the bone. Against: It's a folk song in a streaming era. Ask a teenager; they'll name the Weeknd. Representative government requires representation of the actual people, and "Northwest Passage" is the choice of the already-converted.

The case for "Hallelujah" (Leonard Cohen)

For: The most-covered Canadian song in history; a melody that works in any language; a writer whose very ambivalence about the country is the country's temperament. If the criterion is global reach plus depth, nothing else is close — it's been sung at Olympics, funerals, and talent shows on five continents. Against: It could be anyone's. Stripped of Cohen's gravel, the song travels so far it loses its address. A national song should name the nation, or at least be from it unmistakably — and "Hallelujah" is from everywhere now.

The case for "The Hockey Song" (Stompin' Tom Connors)

For: The most functionally national song ever written — it literally plays in every arena, every winter, and everyone knows every word including the Zamboni verse. No other candidate is actually used as a national song, weekly, by millions. Against: It's about hockey. Delightfully, purely, only about hockey — which makes it the world's greatest sports anthem and, arguably, a specialty item as the country's song.

The case for "Blinding Lights" (The Weeknd)

For: The biggest song of the streaming age by a Canadian; the most-consumed, most-recognized Canadian recording on earth right now. If the national song should be chosen by the actual nation — by what the nation demonstrably plays, streams, and sings — the data has a clear winner. Against: It's about driving fast at night in a relationship apocalypse. Glorious, immortal pop — but it could be from anywhere, says nothing about anywhere, and will the kids in 2076 still... they might! That's the problem with betting against Max Martin? — keep it clean: that's the risk of canonizing anything this young.

The verdict: the question is the answer

Here's the truth the debate keeps revealing: Canada doesn't want one song. The country's whole musical identity is plural by design — the two solitudes, the regions, the eras, the hockey song for the rink and "Hallelujah" for the funeral and "Blinding Lights" for the drive between them. The correct answer to "if Canada had one song" is that the premise breaks on contact: we'd refuse the assignment, politely, and then argue about it at the kitchen party until 2 a.m. Which is, of course, the most Canadian possible outcome. The songs are the argument. The argument is the song.

Frequently asked questions

What is Canada’s unofficial national anthem?

“Northwest Passage” wins critics’ polls and the road-trip category; “The Hockey Song” wins the arena category; “Mon pays” owns Quebec’s heart — see our full debate.

Has “Blinding Lights” become a Canadian classic?

By scale of consumption, yes — it’s the biggest-charting single in Billboard Hot 100 history by any artist, Canadian or not.

Why is there no single Canadian song?

The country’s two-languages, many-regions structure makes pluralism the default — which is why the debate itself, renewed every generation, is the closest thing to an answer Canada has.
