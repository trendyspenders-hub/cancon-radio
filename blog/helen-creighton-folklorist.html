Helen Creighton: The Folklorist Who Saved Nova Scotia's Songs

Somewhere in Nova Scotia, in the decades after the First World War, a woman with an Edison phonograph began driving the province's back roads, knocking on farmhouse doors, and asking strangers to sing into her machine. Helen Creighton spent a lifetime at this — collecting the songs of Nova Scotia's fishermen, farmers, sailors, and lumberjacks at the exact moment the tradition was about to vanish — and in doing so, she saved a cultural inheritance that would otherwise be lost. Nova Scotia's songbook has her name on the copyright of its memory.

Chapter one: the collection begins

Creighton (1899–1989) began collecting in the 1920s, initially informally, then with scholarly purpose: her 1932 book Folk-Songs from Southern New Brunswick announced the mission, and the decades that followed made her Canada's most prolific folk-song collector. Her method was direct and tireless: travel to the communities (coastal villages, island outports, lumber camps, Acadian settlements), gain trust, and record — on wax cylinders first, then discs — whoever had songs. She collected in English, French, and Mi'kmaw; from men and women, elders and children; work songs, love songs, ballads, and the Nova Scotia standards that would define the province's musical identity.

Chapter two: the canon she kept

The Creighton collection includes songs that became Canada's folk backbone: most famously "Farewell to Nova Scotia," the province's unofficial anthem, which she documented in the field and published for the nation; the sea songs and shanties (see our shanty feature); the lumberjack ballads (see our work-songs feature); and hundreds of ballads tracing the British Isles' repertoire as it evolved on North Atlantic soil. Her Traditional Songs from Nova Scotia (1950) remains the standard collection, and her recordings — thousands of them, now preserved in the national archive — are the primary source for anyone studying the region's traditional music. The Mi'kmaw and Acadian material she documented (with collaborators) stands among the earliest serious records of those traditions in the scholarly archive.

Chapter three: the legacy and the reckoning

Creighton's honors were total — the Order of Canada, honorary doctorates, the title of Canada's first lady of folklore — and her legacy has only grown as the archive keeps giving: the folk revival singers of the 60s (see our features) drew directly from her collections, and the current trad revival keeps re-mining them. The modern reckoning with colonial-era collecting (see our First Nations 78s feature) complicates the frame — her Mi'kmaw recordings raise the questions of consent, ownership, and repatriation that the field now confronts — without diminishing the rescue she performed. One woman, one phonograph, one province's songs: the archive is her monument, and the songs sing on.

Why she matters

Creighton matters because collection is creation's first act: without her, the Maritime songbook — the raw material of Stan Rogers, Great Big Sea, and the whole Atlantic revival (see our features) — would be a rumor. She heard the tradition's last living carriers and caught the songs in flight. Nova Scotia sings "Farewell to Nova Scotia" at every farewell. It exists because she knocked on the door.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Helen Creighton?

Canada’s most prolific folk-song collector (1899–1989) — the Nova Scotia folklorist who documented thousands of traditional songs across the Maritimes, including “Farewell to Nova Scotia.”

What songs did Helen Creighton collect?

Her collection spans Nova Scotia’s English, French, and Mi’kmaw traditions — including the province’s signature folk standards, sea songs, and lumberjack ballads.

Why is Helen Creighton important?

She preserved Maritime Canada’s songbook at the moment its living tradition was vanishing — the archive that later fed the folk revival and today’s trad resurgence.
