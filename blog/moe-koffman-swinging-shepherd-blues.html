Moe Koffman: "Swinging Shepherd Blues" and the Sound of Toronto Jazz

Every Canadian city has a musical golden age; Toronto's ran on swing, and its soundtrack had a flute on top. Moe Koffman — flutist, saxophonist, bandleader, and session titan — was the hardest-working horn player in the country's history, and the composer of one immortal melody that escaped jazz entirely to become a standard of Canadian life: "Swinging Shepherd Blues."

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the journeyman (1928–1950s)

Born Morris Koffman in Toronto in 1928, he came up the working-musician way: big bands, dance gigs, endless nights on the bandstand, and a golden-eared facility on both flute and sax that made him the city's first-call player by his twenties. The post-war years made him a recording artist with an ear for the modern: his flute-forward sound rode the cool-jazz wave before the style had a name, and by the mid-50s he was both a nightclub headliner and an in-demand session man — the Toronto studio scene's secret weapon.

Chapter two: the immortal melody (1957)

In 1957, Koffman recorded a composition he'd been developing — a minor-mode, flute-led original? — keep it clean: a memorable original melody that swung like a shepherd's air from the old country — and "Swinging Shepherd Blues" became one of the unlikeliest hits in Canadian music: a jazz instrumental that crossed over to the pop charts at home and abroad, covered endlessly (including by jazz royalty? — keep it clean: including by major international artists), and absorbed into the national songbook. It remains one of the very few Canadian compositions of its era that absolutely everyone has heard, even if they can't name it — the definition of a standard.

Chapter three: the institution

Koffman spent the following four decades being Toronto jazz personified: his long-running band held residencies that became civic institutions; his flute anchored the Rob McConnell Boss Brass — the hardest-swinging big band on the continent — as one of its signature voices; and his session credits sprawled across the country's pop and commercial landscape. Honors followed: the Order of Canada, hall-of-fame inductions, and the universal local affection reserved for people who are a city's sound.

Why he matters

Koffman matters as both composer and cornerstone: "Swinging Shepherd Blues" proved a Canadian instrumental could conquer the pop world, and his five decades of bandstand excellence built the infrastructure — gigs, residencies, standards of playing — that Toronto's jazz scene still stands on. The shepherd swung. The city has never stopped.

Frequently asked questions

What was Moe Koffman’s biggest hit?

“Swinging Shepherd Blues” (1957), the crossover jazz instrumental that became an international standard and a fixture of Canadian life.

What instrument did Moe Koffman play?

Primarily flute and saxophone — he was Toronto’s first-call session player and a longtime voice of the Boss Brass.

What was Moe Koffman’s connection to the Boss Brass?

He was a longtime featured player with Rob McConnell’s Boss Brass, the Toronto big band regarded as one of the era’s finest.
