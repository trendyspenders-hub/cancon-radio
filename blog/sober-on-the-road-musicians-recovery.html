Sober on the Road: Canadian Musicians on Recovery and Touring

The mythology of the road runs on whiskey — but the reality of the road, for a growing number of Canadian musicians, runs on recovery: sobriety maintained in the hardest environment there is. The musicians doing the hardest version of recovery are telling their stories, and the industry is — slowly, belatedly — changing.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the environment

Touring is engineered for addiction: the hours inverted (work at night, sleep by day), the alcohol everywhere (the hospitality rider — the venue's contractual obligation to provide free drinks), the loneliness (six people in a vehicle, everyone on their phone), and the identity (the rock-and-roll self-image that treats excess as authenticity). For musicians in recovery, every element of the job is a trigger, and the job cannot be quit — the touring is the livelihood.

Chapter two: the musicians who did it

Canadian music's recovery stories run from the legends who got sober mid-career and kept the career to the current generation's openness (the younger artists treating sobriety as normal — posting their anniversaries, asking for the sparkling-water rider). The storytelling matters: every musician who says I got sober and the music got better rewrites the mythology for the next kid loading a van. The road still runs on adrenaline. It doesn't have to run on the bottle.

Chapter three: the industry's slow change

The ecosystem is adapting: the sober-friendly green rooms (the hospitality rider finally negotiable), the festival harm-reduction tents, the crew culture shifting (the post-show ritual diversifying), and the conversation itself (the industry-panel confessions, the artist-to-artist support networks). The change is incomplete — the mythology dies hard — but it's real, and it's saving careers and lives. The road is still the road. It's just more survivable now.

Frequently asked questions

How do musicians stay sober while touring?

With structure and support: peer networks, flexible riders, changed rituals, and often telling the crew and the audience — the secrecy being the heaviest load.

Are there sober musicians in Canada?

Many — from mid-career legends to a younger generation treating sobriety as ordinary, several telling their stories publicly.

Is the music industry becoming more recovery-friendly?

Slowly: sober-friendly riders, festival harm-reduction services, and shifting crew culture — real change, still incomplete.
