Jeff Healey: "Angel Eyes" and the Legend of the Lap Guitar

Picture the standard image of the blues guitar hero: sunglasses, swagger, guitar slung high. Now invert the guitar entirely. Jeff Healey — blind from infancy, his instrument laid flat across his lap like a zither, both hands flying over the strings — looked like nothing the blues world had seen, and played like something it had been waiting for. For one dazzling late-80s run, the Toronto kid conquered the world's charts; then, characteristically, he walked away from the formula to chase the music he actually loved.

BY CANCON RADIO2 MIN READAN ORAL HISTORY

Chapter one: the flat guitar

Healey lost his sight to retinoblastoma as an infant, and the disability shaped a technique the sighted world found alien: the guitar flat on the lap, fretted from above, four fingers tapping and bending across a landscape of strings. Raised on blues and jazz records — his father's collection and a childhood obsession with trumpet (his first instrument) — he was a teenage sensation on Toronto's blues circuit, sitting in with the masters and astonishing rooms of people who'd seen everything. A fateful club jam introduced him to the musicians who'd become his band — and to a Stevie Ray Vaughan? — keep it clean: and to the attention that would make him a star.

Chapter two: the detonation (1988–1992)

The debut, See the Light (1988), made Healey a global phenomenon: platinum sales, a Grammy nomination, and "Angel Eyes" — the smoky ballad that hit #1 on the American adult-contemporary charts? — keep it clean: that became a worldwide adult-contemporary smash and remains a permanent soft-rock classic. "Confidence Man" and a scorching cover of "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" followed; the band toured arenas, appeared in the cult classic Road House (playing — of course — a blind blues guitarist), and Healey, barely out of his teens, was carrying a guitar hero's mantle most players spend lifetimes chasing.

Chapter three: the turn

Here's what made Healey more than a moment: he refused to stay inside it. As the blues-rock market cooled, he pivoted — hard — toward his first loves: traditional jazz trumpet and the vintage American songbook. His later records (Mess of Blues, and the jazz projects that consumed his final years) traded stadium gloss for swing-era authenticity, and he became a beloved CBC radio host, sharing his encyclopedic record collection with the country every week. He'd conquered pop on his own terms; then he went exactly where the music called him.

Chapter four: the legacy

Healey died of cancer in 2008, at 41, and the tributes revealed the scale of what the country had lost: a player of staggering technique, a bandleader of generosity, and a figure whose blindness had never once been the most interesting thing about him. The Jeff Healey Foundation continues his work supporting music education for the blind and visually impaired — the most fitting memorial imaginable. And "Angel Eyes" endures, as do the live tapes: proof that a kid from Toronto, playing guitar the "wrong" way, belonged on the world's biggest stages from the first note.

Frequently asked questions

How did Jeff Healey play guitar?

With the guitar flat on his lap, fretted from above with all four fingers — a technique developed in childhood after losing his sight to retinoblastoma.

What was Jeff Healey’s biggest hit?

“Angel Eyes” (1988), the worldwide adult-contemporary smash from his platinum debut See the Light.

What did Jeff Healey do after the 80s?

He pivoted to his first love — traditional jazz as a trumpeter and vintage-song interpreter — while hosting a beloved blues radio show, until his death in 2008.
