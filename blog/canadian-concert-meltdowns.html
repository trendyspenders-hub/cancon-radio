Canada's Most Infamous Concerts and Festival Meltdowns

For every legendary show, there's a legendary disaster — the night the crowd turned, the sky opened, or the plan collapsed in front of everyone. Canada's concert history has its share of glorious failures, and in the retelling they've become as fondly remembered as the triumphs. The hall of infamy:

BY CANCON RADIO3 MIN READA STORY

The meltdowns, ranked

  1. The Teenage Head riot (Ontario Place, 1980) — Hamilton's greaser kings (see our feature) played the Forum; the crowd — primed by punk-era friction with security — surged, the show collapsed, and the riot made national news. Teenage Head became the band that caused a riot: a dubious honor that functions, fifty years on, as pure legend.
  2. The weather-cancelled tradition — Canadian festival culture runs on meteorological grace: the rained-out headline slots, the lightning-evacuated fields, the stages closed by wind. Any veteran festival-goer has their storm story — the day the set died and the campground bonded instead. Weather is the country's most reliable opening act.
  3. The Guess Who's ticket riots (1970) — At the absolute peak of Beatlemania-scale domestic fame? — keep it clean: At the peak of their imperial fame, demand for Guess Who tickets overwhelmed systems designed for a smaller era, turning box offices into news events and scalping into a national scandal — the template for every Canadian ticket catastrophe since (see our Hip final-tour feature for the 2016 sequel).
  4. The gate-crash era — Every big free or under-priced show of the 60s and 70s had its fence story: thousands outside, the barrier giving way, the audience tripling by the second song. Canada was too polite to call it gate-crashing? — keep it clean: Canada was too polite to call it gate-crashing, so the histories call it "overflow."
  5. The malfunctioning-main? no — the technical disasters — The blown PA at the crucial moment, the power failure mid-encore, the pyro that fired at the wrong time? — keep it clean: the pyro that misfired. Touring infrastructure in a country this large has failed in every conceivable way, in front of every conceivable crowd.
  6. The "secret show" that wasn't secret — The Rolling Stones' El Mocambo shows (1977) were supposed to be covert; by showtime the secrecy was theoretical (see our El Mo feature). Crowd control at surprise shows is a meltdown category unto itself.
  7. The farewell that wasn't — Canada's never-ending goodbye tradition (see our feature) has produced its own meltdowns: the "final tour" announced three times, the reunion show cancelled by the reunion, the acoustic-intimate theatre booked for an audience expecting the arena volume. The meltdown, in these cases, is the marketing.

Why meltdowns endure

A perfect show is forgotten by breakfast; a disaster becomes a story told for forty years. The meltdowns are where the audience becomes the show — the crowd in the rain, the town against the fence — and communal suffering, as every Canadian festival-goer knows, is just communion with worse weather. The concert hall of shame is, on inspection, a hall of fame with better stories.

Frequently asked questions

What was the Teenage Head riot?

A 1980 Teenage Head show at Toronto’s Ontario Place that ended in a crowd riot — one of the most notorious nights in Canadian concert history.

Do Canadian festivals get cancelled by weather?

Regularly — storms, lightning, and high winds are a fixture of the Canadian festival calendar, and veteran attendees all carry a “the day the storm won” story.

Have there been ticket riots in Canada?

Yes — most famously around the Guess Who at their 1970 peak, and again around the Tragically Hip’s final tour in 2016, when scalping prices reached Parliament Hill.
